The Lord of the Flies by William Golding

November 10, 2026

You know what happens when you leave a group of little children unattended? Honestly, if you have ever spent time around young children, you probably know that they can be quite a handful. You turn your back for a minute, and suddenly something has been broken, the walls have become their personal painting canvas, and you are left wondering how so much chaos was created in such a short amount of time.

Now, imagine leaving a group of children completely on their own, without parents, teachers, rules, or anyone to supervise them. What happens when there is nobody to tell them what is right and wrong? What happens when the usual structures of society disappear?

The Lord of the Flies by William Golding (Image Source)

Well, that is essentially the situation explored in William Golding's The Lord of the Flies. The novel follows a group of British schoolboys who become stranded on an uninhabited island after a plane crash. There are no adults around, and the children are forced to organise themselves, find food, establish rules, and figure out how to survive.

Initially, they attempt to create some kind of organised society. They elect a leader, establish rules, and try to maintain order. However, things gradually begin to fall apart. Their disagreements become increasingly serious, friendships turn into rivalries, and the desire for power eventually gives way to cruelty, violence, and savagery.

What makes this novel particularly disturbing is that the characters are children. We often associate childhood with innocence, kindness, and simplicity. But Golding presents a much darker possibility. Children are not automatically incapable of cruelty simply because they are young. They can bully, humiliate, manipulate, exclude, and hurt one another, sometimes without even understanding the seriousness of their actions.

And that is what makes The Lord of the Flies such an uncomfortable novel. It is not simply about a group of children losing their way on an island. It is about what happens when social structures weaken, when people stop questioning their actions, and when the desire to belong becomes stronger than the ability to distinguish between right and wrong.

Another thing I find particularly interesting is how recognisable the characters are. Almost every group has people who resemble Ralph, Piggy, Simon, Jack, or Roger in some way. Their personalities represent different aspects of human behaviour, and their interactions reveal how quickly an ordinary group can develop into something deeply unpleasant.

I think the most disturbing part of this novel is not necessarily the violence itself. It is how gradually that violence becomes normalised.

Sometimes, the frightening thing about human nature is not how quickly people become cruel, but how easily they learn to accept cruelty when everyone around them is doing the same thing.

About the Author

William Golding was an English novelist, playwright, and poet born on 19th September 1911 in Cornwall, England. He is widely recognised for his exploration of human nature, civilisation, morality, violence, and the darker aspects of human psychology.

Golding served in the Royal Navy during the Second World War, an experience that influenced his understanding of human violence and the destructive possibilities of human behaviour. His experiences during the war contributed to the pessimistic view of human nature that can be seen in much of his writing.

The Lord of the Flies, published in 1954, is his best-known novel. It explores what happens when a group of schoolboys are left without adult supervision on a deserted island. Through their gradual transformation, Golding examines the conflict between civilisation and savagery.

The novel was initially not an immediate commercial success, but it gradually became one of the most widely studied works of modern English literature. It is particularly important for its psychological exploration of human nature and its criticism of the assumption that civilisation is something permanent or guaranteed.

William Golding received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1983. He also received the Booker Prize in 1980 for his novel Rites of Passage.

He died on 19th June 1993. His works continue to be read and discussed because they raise uncomfortable questions about morality, power, violence, and what lies beneath the surface of seemingly civilised human behaviour.

A Quick Summary: What Is The Lord of the Flies About?

The Lord of the Flies by William Golding is a novel about a group of British schoolboys who become stranded on an uninhabited island after their plane crashes. With no adults to supervise them, the boys must find a way to survive and organise their new lives.

Among the first boys to emerge as important figures are Ralph and Piggy. Ralph discovers a conch shell and uses it to summon the other boys. During their first assembly, the children decide to elect a leader, and Ralph is chosen. Jack, who leads a group of choirboys, becomes responsible for hunting.

Initially, the boys try to maintain some semblance of civilisation. They establish rules, organise meetings, and attempt to keep a signal fire burning so that a passing ship might notice them and rescue them.

However, the group gradually becomes divided. Ralph believes that maintaining the fire, building shelters, and organising their activities should be their priorities. Jack becomes increasingly obsessed with hunting and gaining power. His interest in hunting eventually develops into a desire for dominance.

As the boys become more frightened by rumours of a mysterious beast on the island, their behaviour changes. Jack uses their fear to strengthen his influence and establish his own group. His followers become increasingly savage, and hunting gradually turns into a form of violent entertainment.

Simon, one of the most thoughtful and naturally compassionate boys, eventually discovers that the supposed beast is not an external monster. It is actually the dead body of a parachutist that has become entangled in the trees.

However, before Simon can explain what he has discovered, he is mistaken for the beast and brutally killed by the boys during a frenzied ritualistic dance.

Piggy, who represents reason, intelligence, and rational thinking, also becomes a victim of the growing violence. Roger deliberately releases a massive rock that strikes Piggy, killing him and destroying the conch shell.

By this point, Jack's group has almost completely abandoned the rules and principles that initially governed their behaviour. Ralph becomes the target of a hunt, and the boys eventually set fire to large portions of the island in an attempt to flush him out.

Ironically, the enormous fire attracts the attention of a British naval officer, who arrives on the island and rescues the surviving boys.

The novel ends with Ralph crying over the loss of innocence and the darkness of human nature. What began as an attempt to establish a peaceful society has turned into a horrifying demonstration of cruelty, violence, and the collapse of civilisation.

1. The Characters: Different Personalities, Different Sides of Human Nature

One of the things I particularly like about The Lord of the Flies is how the characters represent different attitudes towards life, society, morality, and power.

If you look at the novel from the perspective of an ordinary group of friends, you can almost recognise these personalities in different people around you. There is someone who tries to organise everyone, someone who comes up with practical solutions, someone who is naturally kind, someone who wants to dominate the group, and someone who enjoys exercising cruelty.

The interesting thing is that these personalities do not simply remain individual characteristics. As the story progresses, they begin influencing the behaviour of the entire group.

Ralph: The Leader and the Voice of Democracy

Ralph represents order, democratic leadership, and the attempt to preserve civilisation. He is elected chief because the other boys find him confident and capable of organising them.

His priorities are relatively practical. He wants the boys to build shelters, maintain the signal fire, establish rules, and work together so that they can eventually be rescued.

What I find interesting about Ralph is that he does not necessarily want power for its own sake. He sees leadership as a responsibility. He understands that the boys need some kind of organisation if they are going to survive.

However, he is not a perfect leader. He sometimes struggles to maintain authority, and his own behaviour is not always consistent with the principles he represents. Nevertheless, he continues to defend the importance of rules and collective responsibility.

In many ways, Ralph represents the part of human society that believes people can live together peacefully through cooperation, discipline, and shared responsibility.

Piggy: Intelligence, Science, and Rational Thinking

Piggy is perhaps one of the saddest characters in the novel. He is intelligent, practical, and often the person who comes up with the most sensible suggestions. He understands the importance of organisation and repeatedly tries to make the other boys think rationally.

He is the one who recognises the usefulness of the conch shell as a means of gathering everyone and establishing rules about who gets to speak. His glasses are also used to concentrate sunlight and create fire, making them essential for the boys' survival.

In a way, Piggy represents science, logic, rationality, and intellectual understanding. He is the person who tries to solve problems through reasoning rather than aggression.

But there is something particularly heartbreaking about his character. The other boys constantly make fun of his physical appearance, his weight, his asthma, and his inability to participate in their physical activities.

Even his real name is never revealed. He is almost entirely identified through the humiliating nickname Piggy, which follows him throughout the novel.

What makes this even more disturbing is that Ralph, who is supposed to represent order and civilisation, also participates in mocking him during the early stages of the story.

This is an important detail because it shows that cruelty does not necessarily begin with the most violent person in a group. Sometimes, ordinary people participate in humiliation simply because everyone else is doing it.

Piggy is eventually killed when Roger deliberately releases a boulder that strikes him. His death, along with the destruction of the conch, represents the collapse of rationality and the social order that Ralph has been trying to preserve.

I find Piggy particularly tragic because even if you remove the island, the survival situation, and all the extreme violence, his experiences of bullying, humiliation, exclusion, and dehumanisation are still painfully recognisable.

Simon: Natural Goodness and Moral Understanding

Simon is another particularly interesting character. Unlike Jack, he does not seem interested in gaining power or controlling the other boys. He is naturally compassionate, thoughtful, and sensitive towards the people around him.

He helps the younger boys, shows concern for others, and often understands things that the rest of the group fails to recognise.

Simon also discovers the truth about the supposed beast. During his encounter with the pig's head, which becomes known as the Lord of the Flies, he realises that the real source of evil is not some external creature hiding on the island.

The darkness the boys fear is actually connected to human nature itself.

Unfortunately, Simon is unable to communicate this truth to the others. In a particularly tragic scene, the boys mistake him for the beast during their frenzied dance and kill him.

His death is significant because the person who comes closest to understanding the truth is destroyed by the very group that needs to hear it.

Jack: Power, Manipulation, and Savagery

Jack is perhaps the most obvious representation of the darker side of human behaviour. Initially, he is the leader of the choirboys and is accustomed to exercising authority over others.

However, he becomes increasingly obsessed with hunting, power, and control. As the novel progresses, he begins rejecting the rules established by Ralph and develops his own group.

What makes Jack particularly dangerous is his ability to manipulate the other boys. He understands their fears, appeals to their desire for excitement, and gradually convinces them that hunting and following him are more important than maintaining order.

He uses the fear of the beast to strengthen his position. Instead of helping the boys understand their fears, he turns those fears into a source of authority.

His transformation demonstrates how easily leadership can become authoritarian when power is valued more than responsibility.

Roger: Cruelty Without Empathy

Roger is another extremely disturbing character. While Jack is interested in power and dominance, Roger appears to take particular pleasure in cruelty.

Early in the novel, he throws stones near a younger boy, deliberately avoiding hitting him because the restrictions of his previous civilisation still influence his behaviour.

But as the authority of society gradually disappears, those restrictions become weaker. Roger eventually becomes increasingly violent and sadistic.

He is responsible for Piggy's death and becomes one of the most frightening figures in Jack's group.

I think Roger represents something particularly uncomfortable: what happens when someone no longer feels the need to restrain their cruel impulses because they believe there will be no consequences.

2. Group Mentality: How Ordinary People Become Part of Something Terrible

This is probably one of the most important themes of the novel for me.

The boys do not all begin as equally violent or aggressive individuals. Some are more interested in maintaining order, some simply want to have fun, and others are frightened by the uncertainty of their situation.

But gradually, the group begins changing. Their individual decisions become increasingly influenced by what everyone else is doing.

Some boys support Ralph because they believe in rules, organisation, and the possibility of rescue. Others begin following Jack because he offers excitement, protection, hunting, and a sense of belonging.

Eventually, the distinction between individual responsibility and group behaviour becomes increasingly blurred.

Think about the way Jack gradually convinces the boys to abandon their original responsibilities. The signal fire becomes less important, the rules become less meaningful, and hunting becomes a central part of their identity.

Even boys who might not have independently committed certain acts of violence become involved when they are surrounded by others behaving in the same way.

This is what makes group mentality so dangerous. People sometimes stop evaluating their actions independently. Instead, they begin accepting behaviour simply because it has become normal within their group.

And once that happens, doing something wrong can become surprisingly easy.

You might initially feel uncomfortable participating in something cruel. But if everyone around you is laughing, encouraging it, and treating it as perfectly acceptable, you may eventually stop questioning it.

I think Golding illustrates this particularly effectively through the boys' transformation into Jack's tribe. Their collective behaviour becomes increasingly aggressive, and individual moral judgement gradually disappears.

The tragedy is that many of them do not seem to recognise how far they have moved away from the principles they initially believed in.

3. The Lord of the Flies: What Does the Title Actually Mean?

Now, coming to the title, I think this is one of the most interesting aspects of the novel.

The expression Lord of the Flies is associated with Beelzebub, a name traditionally connected with the devil. The title therefore introduces the idea of evil, corruption, and the darker aspects of human existence.

Within the novel, the Lord of the Flies is represented by the severed head of a pig that Jack and his hunters place on a sharpened stick as an offering to the beast.

As the head begins to decay, it becomes covered with flies. This gruesome image gives the novel its title and becomes one of its most important symbols.

But what makes this symbol particularly interesting is that the Lord of the Flies is not simply an external monster.

During Simon's encounter with the pig's head, he experiences a disturbing revelation. The beast is not something that exists independently of the boys. The real source of their fear and violence lies within human nature itself.

In other words, there is no mysterious creature that is responsible for all the terrible things happening on the island. The boys themselves are gradually becoming the source of the evil they are so frightened of.

Jack and his followers kill animals, intimidate others, abandon their responsibilities, and eventually participate in the deaths of Simon and Piggy.

The Lord of the Flies therefore becomes a symbol of the darkness that exists within human beings.

I think this is one of the most powerful ideas in the entire novel. People often imagine evil as something that comes from outside. We look for monsters, villains, and external threats. But Golding suggests that the capacity for cruelty is already present within human beings.

And perhaps the most frightening thing is that we do not always recognise it when it begins to emerge.

4. Civilization vs Savagery: What Happens When There Are No Rules?

Another major theme of the novel is the conflict between civilisation and savagery.

When the boys first arrive on the island, they attempt to recreate the social structures they are familiar with. They organise meetings, elect a leader, establish rules, and assign responsibilities.

The conch shell becomes an important symbol of this organised society. It gives the boys a way to gather together and establishes a system in which everyone has an opportunity to speak.

Ralph understands that the group needs rules if it is going to function properly. He repeatedly tries to remind the boys that maintaining the signal fire and building shelters are essential.

However, Jack gradually becomes more interested in hunting and controlling the group. He begins rejecting the authority of the conch and eventually establishes a separate tribe.

As the novel progresses, the conch loses its influence, the signal fire becomes neglected, and the boys become increasingly violent.

What I find particularly interesting is that civilisation in the novel is not presented as something permanent. It requires constant effort, cooperation, and a willingness to respect certain boundaries.

The moment the boys stop recognising the importance of those boundaries, their behaviour begins changing.

I would not say that the novel suggests every human being automatically becomes cruel in the absence of rules. That would be an oversimplification. Rather, it shows how easily existing restraints can weaken when there is no accountability, when fear takes over, and when individuals begin justifying their actions.

The absence of civilisation does not necessarily create evil from nothing. It can allow destructive impulses that were previously restrained to become much more visible.

5. Piggy and the Reality of Bullying: Why This Part Feels So Realistic

One of the saddest aspects of The Lord of the Flies is how realistically it portrays bullying.

We sometimes assume that children are naturally innocent and kind. There is a tendency to believe that cruelty is something people develop only when they become adults.

But anyone who has spent enough time around children knows that this is not necessarily true.

Children can be extremely unkind to one another. They can make cruel remarks about someone's appearance, physical abilities, background, intelligence, or personality. They can deliberately exclude someone, create humiliating nicknames, and turn another person's insecurities into entertainment.

And sometimes, they do not even understand how deeply their words can hurt someone.

Piggy's character reflects this reality particularly well. He is repeatedly mocked because of his physical appearance and health problems. His nickname becomes a permanent part of his identity, and the other boys rarely make an effort to see him as an individual beyond that label.

This is what I find particularly disturbing. Piggy is not simply bullied because he is different. He is gradually treated as though his feelings, opinions, and dignity matter less than those of the other boys.

That is dehumanisation.

And the process of dehumanisation often begins with seemingly ordinary things. A cruel nickname, a joke at someone's expense, a repeated insult, or the habit of treating one person as though they are somehow less worthy of respect.

Once a group begins seeing someone as an object of ridicule rather than as another human being, it becomes much easier to justify further cruelty.

What makes Piggy's character particularly tragic is that he continues trying to reason with the other boys even when they repeatedly humiliate him. His intelligence is useful to the group, but his actual presence is rarely respected.

And this is why I think his character remains relevant even if we remove the island, the conch, and the extreme circumstances of the novel. Bullying and social exclusion do not require a deserted island. They can happen in classrooms, friendship groups, workplaces, and ordinary social settings.

6. Fear as a Tool of Manipulation: How Jack Gains Control

Another interesting aspect of the novel is the way Jack uses fear to establish his authority.

The boys are frightened by the possibility that a beast exists somewhere on the island. They do not know what it is, where it might be hiding, or whether it is capable of harming them.

This uncertainty creates anxiety throughout the group.

Instead of encouraging the boys to examine their fears rationally, Jack uses the situation to strengthen his position. He presents himself as someone who can protect them and promises that his hunters will deal with the beast.

Gradually, his authority becomes connected to the fear that he is supposedly protecting them from.

I think this is a particularly important observation about human behaviour. When people are frightened, they sometimes become more willing to surrender their judgement to someone who promises security.

They may stop asking questions, overlook contradictions, and accept increasingly extreme behaviour because they believe that someone else knows what needs to be done.

Jack understands that fear can bring people together, but he uses that unity to gain power rather than to protect the group.

And that is what makes manipulation so dangerous. People do not always follow someone because they genuinely agree with everything that person says. Sometimes, they follow because they are frightened, confused, or desperate to feel protected.

7. The Conch Shell and the Signal Fire: Symbols of Order and Hope

Two particularly important symbols in the novel are the conch shell and the signal fire.

The conch represents authority, democratic participation, communication, and the rules that initially hold the group together.

During meetings, the conch gives the boys a way to organise their discussions. Whoever holds it is allowed to speak, and the others are expected to listen.

In this sense, the conch represents more than a simple object. It represents the idea that people can coexist by recognising shared rules and respecting one another's voices.

However, as Jack's influence grows, the conch gradually loses its authority. The boys stop respecting the system it represents, and eventually it is destroyed when Piggy is killed.

The destruction of the conch symbolises the complete collapse of the democratic order that Ralph has been trying to maintain.

The signal fire, meanwhile, represents hope and the possibility of rescue. It is the boys' connection to the outside world and their chance of returning to civilisation.

Ralph repeatedly insists that the fire must be maintained because he understands that survival is not simply about hunting or finding food. They need to remain visible to passing ships.

But the boys gradually become distracted by hunting and their internal conflicts. Their priorities change, and the signal fire is repeatedly neglected.

Interestingly, it is ultimately a fire started by Jack's tribe to flush Ralph out of hiding that attracts the attention of a passing naval officer.

This creates an ironic ending: the destructive fire that represents the boys' complete descent into savagery becomes the very thing that leads to their rescue.

8. The Ending: When the Entire Island Becomes a Hunting Ground

The ending of the novel is particularly disturbing because it shows just how far the boys have moved away from their original attempts at civilisation.

By this stage, Jack's group has become extremely violent. Piggy is dead, Simon has been killed, and Ralph has become the final major obstacle to Jack's complete control.

Instead of simply disagreeing with Ralph or allowing him to leave, Jack and his followers decide to hunt him down.

They set fire to the island in an attempt to force him out of hiding. The entire situation becomes an organised attempt to kill another child.

What makes this particularly frightening is that the boys are no longer behaving as though they are participating in a disagreement or a childish game. They have become completely absorbed in the violence of the hunt.

Ralph is eventually rescued when a British naval officer arrives on the island after noticing the smoke from the fire.

The arrival of the officer suddenly brings the boys face to face with the civilisation they had left behind. Their violent behaviour is interrupted, and they are confronted with the reality of what has happened.

Ralph begins crying, not simply because he has been rescued, but because he recognises the loss of innocence and the terrible things that have taken place.

The ending is particularly effective because it does not offer a completely comforting resolution. The boys are rescued, but the experience they have gone through cannot simply be erased.

And there is another interesting irony here. While the boys have been descending into savagery on the island, the adult world outside is also engaged in a devastating war.

The naval officer represents civilisation, but he belongs to a world that is itself involved in large-scale violence. The novel therefore makes us question whether the distinction between the supposedly civilised adult world and the savage behaviour of the children is as straightforward as it first appears.

9. The Absence of Consequences: Why People Sometimes Stop Restraining Themselves

This is another aspect of the novel that I find particularly interesting.

Think about how people behave when they believe that nobody is watching them or that they will not be held responsible for their actions.

In ordinary society, there are certain boundaries that people are expected to respect. There are rules, laws, social expectations, and consequences for deliberately hurting others.

These structures do not make everyone kind or moral, but they do create restrictions on what people can openly do.

On the island, those restrictions gradually disappear. There are no parents, teachers, police officers, or other adults to intervene when the boys behave cruelly.

As a result, some of them begin acting in ways they might previously have been reluctant to consider.

Roger is a particularly clear example of this transformation. At first, he throws stones near a younger boy but deliberately avoids hitting him. The invisible restrictions of his previous social environment are still present.

Later, as the group becomes increasingly violent and the fear of punishment disappears, he becomes much more openly cruel.

I think this reflects an important aspect of human psychology. Sometimes, people refrain from doing something wrong not necessarily because they have developed a strong sense of empathy, but because they know there will be consequences.

The moment they believe that nobody can hold them accountable, it becomes easier to justify their actions.

Of course, not everyone behaves this way. There are people who maintain their moral principles even in extremely difficult circumstances. Simon is an important example of that possibility.

But Golding reminds us that morality cannot simply depend on external restrictions. It also requires an internal willingness to recognise the humanity of other people.

10. Is Evil Something We Learn, or Is It Already Within Us?

This is perhaps the biggest philosophical question raised by the novel.

We often like to believe that human beings are naturally good and that cruelty is something introduced by society, circumstances, or external influences.

But The Lord of the Flies challenges this comfortable assumption.

The boys do not arrive on the island as completely different creatures. They are ordinary schoolchildren who gradually become involved in increasingly disturbing behaviour.

Their transformation suggests that human beings have the capacity for both cooperation and destruction.

Ralph attempts to preserve order. Piggy tries to reason with the group. Simon demonstrates compassion and understanding. Jack pursues power, while Roger becomes increasingly cruel.

All these different personalities exist within the same environment, which is precisely what makes the novel so interesting.

It would be too simplistic to say that Golding believes everyone is inherently evil. The novel is more complicated than that. It explores the tension between the social structures that restrain destructive behaviour and the darker impulses that can emerge when those structures weaken.

I think the most important lesson is that being civilised is not simply about living in a society with laws and rules. It is also about developing the ability to question ourselves, recognise the consequences of our actions, and treat other people with dignity even when there is no immediate reward for doing so.

11. A Modern Interpretation: Bullying, Social Groups, and the Pressure to Conform

Although The Lord of the Flies was published in 1954, I think many of its ideas remain relevant today.

You do not need to be stranded on an island to experience group mentality, bullying, manipulation, or the pressure to follow what everyone else is doing.

Consider an ordinary school or college environment. There may be different friendship groups, popular students, people who dominate conversations, and individuals who are repeatedly excluded or mocked.

Sometimes, a particular person becomes the target of jokes. Others join in, perhaps because they want to fit in or because they are afraid of becoming the next target themselves.

Gradually, the group develops an attitude in which humiliating that individual becomes acceptable.

This is very similar to what happens to Piggy. The other boys repeatedly reduce him to his physical appearance and nickname, and very few of them make an effort to challenge the behaviour.

The same kind of group pressure can also be seen in online spaces. A single person can become the target of hundreds of comments, jokes, insults, and humiliating remarks. People who might never behave that way in a face-to-face interaction can become much more aggressive when they are part of a crowd.

Another important connection is the way people sometimes accept ideas without questioning them. If someone influential within a group makes a claim, others may begin repeating it simply because it has become popular.

This is why independent thinking matters so much. The ability to question an idea, disagree with a group, and recognise when something is wrong is an important part of maintaining our moral judgement.

I think this novel is particularly relevant here because it shows that the danger does not always come from one exceptionally cruel individual. It can also come from the willingness of ordinary people to participate, remain silent, or simply follow the crowd.

And sometimes, the person who refuses to participate in that behaviour is the one who needs the most courage.

💜 Final Thoughts

The Lord of the Flies is an incredibly dark novel. There is no doubt about that. It explores violence, cruelty, bullying, manipulation, the desire for power, and the gradual collapse of civilisation.

But what makes it particularly memorable is how realistic certain aspects of the story feel, even though the situation itself is quite extreme.

We may never find ourselves stranded on a deserted island with a group of children, but the behaviour Golding describes is not entirely unfamiliar. Bullying, social exclusion, cruel nicknames, group pressure, manipulation, and the tendency to follow influential individuals are all things that can be observed in ordinary society.

Piggy's character is particularly important to me in this regard. His experiences show how easily a person can become the target of repeated humiliation simply because they are different from everyone else. His intelligence and practical suggestions are useful to the group, but the other boys repeatedly fail to recognise his dignity as an individual.

Simon's character is equally tragic. He understands something that the others fail to recognise, but his attempt to communicate the truth ends in his death. It is a disturbing reminder of how easily fear and collective aggression can destroy someone who is trying to make others understand.

I also find the novel's treatment of group mentality particularly important. People sometimes become so invested in belonging to a group that they stop questioning its behaviour. They accept ideas without examining them and participate in actions they might otherwise have considered unacceptable.

And perhaps that is one of the most important things we can take away from this novel: the importance of independent thinking. The moment we stop questioning our actions, the moment we begin treating cruelty as something normal, and the moment we allow a group to make our moral decisions for us, we become vulnerable to some very destructive behaviour.

I would also say that the novel challenges the idea that childhood automatically guarantees innocence. Children need guidance, boundaries, and an understanding of empathy. Kindness and respect are not qualities that simply appear without being nurtured.

What I particularly appreciate about Golding's writing is that he manages to explore these complicated psychological and philosophical questions through a relatively straightforward survival story. The island becomes a small representation of society, and the boys become a way of examining the different forces that influence human behaviour.

I would definitely recommend The Lord of the Flies if you enjoy psychological fiction, philosophical questions, literary symbolism, and novels that examine the darker aspects of human nature.

It is not necessarily a comfortable read, but I think that is precisely what makes it worth reading. It leaves you questioning how much of our behaviour is shaped by social structures, how easily people can be influenced by groups, and whether morality can survive when nobody is around to enforce it.
