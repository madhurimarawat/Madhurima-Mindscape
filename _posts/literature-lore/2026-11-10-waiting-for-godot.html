Waiting for Godot by Samuel Beckett

November 10, 2026

Waiting for Godot by Samuel Beckett is one of the most unusual plays I have come across. If you are familiar with the Theatre of the Absurd, you probably already know that it deals with ideas such as circular plots, meaningless existence, uncertainty, and the repeated search for meaning in a world that does not necessarily provide any answers.

And this play is almost a perfect example of that. Two characters, Vladimir and Estragon, spend the play waiting for someone named Godot. They talk, argue, think about leaving, consider their situation, and continue waiting. But Godot never arrives.

Waiting for Godot by Samuel Beckett (Source)

Now, if you read the play for the first time, you might genuinely wonder what is happening. Even when you watch a performance, the characters and their conversations can feel strange. There is no conventional storyline, no clear destination, and no satisfying resolution in the way we usually expect from a play.

But I think one of the most interesting ways to understand this play is to think about something very ordinary: preparing for competitive examinations.

Imagine someone who keeps appearing for the same competitive examination every year. They study, prepare, give the exam, wait for the result, and hope that this time they will finally get selected. But perhaps the examination gets postponed, the result is delayed, they do not clear the cutoff, or something unexpected happens.

And then they begin the entire process again. They prepare for another attempt, give another examination, and wait for another result. In this analogy, Godot becomes the result, the final selection, or the opportunity that they have been waiting for all along.

The interesting part is that the person does not know exactly when that result will arrive. They do not even know whether it will arrive in the way they expect. Still, they keep waiting because they believe something might happen tomorrow.

And that, for me, is one of the most beautiful ways of understanding Waiting for Godot. It is not necessarily about waiting for one particular person. It is about waiting for something that we believe will finally give our lives direction, meaning, or a sense of completion.

Godot could represent hope, God, salvation, a better future, a job, an opportunity, or simply the answer we keep expecting from life. The play never gives us a definite explanation, and perhaps that uncertainty is precisely the point.

What makes it even more interesting is that Vladimir and Estragon are very different individuals, yet they are caught in the same situation. One appears more thoughtful and philosophical, while the other is more concerned with immediate physical discomfort and everyday needs.

The play also introduces Pozzo and Lucky, whose relationship brings questions of power, control, dependence, and submission into the story.

So, although the play may initially seem like two people doing nothing while waiting for someone who never arrives, it actually opens up several questions about human existence. And the more I think about it, the more I realise how many ordinary situations in life resemble this endless waiting.

Sometimes, we spend so much of our lives waiting for something to happen that we forget to question what we are doing while we wait.

About the Author

Samuel Beckett was an Irish novelist, playwright, and poet born in 1906. He is one of the most important writers associated with modernist literature and the Theatre of the Absurd.

His writing often explores human existence, uncertainty, isolation, suffering, and the difficulty of finding meaning in life. His works are particularly known for their unusual dramatic structures, minimal settings, repetitive conversations, and characters who often find themselves trapped in situations they cannot fully understand or escape.

Some of his important works include Waiting for Godot, Endgame, Krapp's Last Tape, and Happy Days. He also wrote novels such as Molloy, Malone Dies, and The Unnamable.

Waiting for Godot, originally written in French as En attendant Godot, is his best-known play. It became one of the defining works of the Theatre of the Absurd and received international recognition for its unconventional approach to drama.

A Quick Summary: What Is Waiting for Godot About?

Waiting for Godot is a two-act play by Samuel Beckett that follows two characters, Vladimir and Estragon, who are waiting beside a tree for someone named Godot.

While waiting, they talk about different things, argue, remember fragments of their lives, consider leaving, and repeatedly return to the fact that they are waiting for Godot. However, Godot does not appear.

During their wait, they encounter two other characters: Pozzo and Lucky. Pozzo is a man who controls Lucky, his servant, using a rope attached around Lucky's neck. Their relationship introduces another dimension to the play, particularly questions of power, dependence, and human submission.

In the second act, Vladimir and Estragon return to the same place and continue waiting. Their conversations and experiences repeat many of the patterns from the first act. Pozzo and Lucky also return, but their circumstances have changed. Pozzo has become blind, while Lucky can no longer speak.

A boy eventually arrives with a message from Godot. He says that Godot will not come that day but will supposedly arrive the following day. Vladimir and Estragon therefore decide to return and wait again.

At the end, they talk about leaving, but neither of them moves. The play closes with them still waiting.

The most unusual part is that Godot never actually appears. We never receive a definite explanation of who he is or why the two men are waiting for him.

And that is what makes the play so fascinating. Its central action is not a journey towards a clear destination. It is the act of waiting itself.

1. Theatre of the Absurd: Why Does This Play Feel So Strange?

The first thing we need to understand about Waiting for Godot is its literary classification. It is one of the most important examples of the Theatre of the Absurd.

Now, what exactly is the Theatre of the Absurd? It is a form of drama that challenges the conventional expectations of storytelling. Instead of presenting a clear plot with a beginning, middle, and satisfying conclusion, absurdist plays often explore the uncertainty and apparent meaninglessness of human existence.

The characters may repeat conversations, perform seemingly pointless actions, wait for something that never happens, or struggle to understand the world around them.

This is exactly what happens in Waiting for Godot. Vladimir and Estragon spend most of their time waiting. They talk about leaving, but they do not leave. They expect Godot, but he never arrives. They receive a message that he will come tomorrow, and then the same situation begins again.

There is no conventional dramatic progress in which one event naturally leads to a final resolution. Instead, the play creates a sense of repetition and uncertainty.

This does not mean that the play is simply meaningless or that nothing can be interpreted from it. In fact, its unusual structure allows us to think about some very serious questions.

Why do human beings continue waiting? Why do we keep hoping for something when we have no guarantee that it will happen? And what happens when the things we expect to give our lives meaning never arrive?

These are some of the questions that make the play an important work of absurdist drama.

2. Godot as the Result of a Competitive Examination

This is probably my favourite way of understanding Waiting for Godot. I think the entire situation becomes much easier to relate to when we connect it with competitive examinations and the long process of waiting for a result.

Imagine a student preparing for a competitive examination. They study for months, sometimes years. They appear for the examination, wait for the answer key, wait for the result, and hope that they will finally get selected.

But what happens if they do not clear the examination? They prepare again. Perhaps the next examination gets postponed. Perhaps the result is delayed. Perhaps the cutoff is higher than expected. Or perhaps they reach the final stage but still do not get selected.

And then the cycle begins again. Another year, another examination, another result, and another period of waiting.

In this situation, Godot becomes the result or the final selection that the student has been expecting. It is something that might change their life, but they have no complete control over when it will happen.

Sometimes they might think that the result will come tomorrow. Sometimes they might wonder whether the examination has been cancelled. At other times, they might even begin thinking about the possibility that they will never get selected.

Yet, despite all these uncertainties, they continue preparing and waiting because giving up is not always an easy option.

This is very similar to Vladimir and Estragon's situation. They keep waiting because they believe Godot might arrive. Even when he does not come, the possibility of his arrival tomorrow gives them a reason to return.

I think this analogy also helps us understand why the play is not limited to one particular situation. Godot does not have to represent an examination result. He can represent any outcome that we keep expecting from life.

3. Waiting for Something: Jobs, Degrees, Health, and Everyday Life

The more I think about this play, the more I realise that the idea of waiting can be connected to almost every part of human life.

Think about someone who is dealing with a medical condition. They visit doctors, take medicines, undergo tests, and wait to see whether their health will improve. Sometimes the treatment works. Sometimes it takes longer. Sometimes they have to try something else.

They continue taking medicines and following different treatments without knowing exactly when they will feel better. The expected recovery becomes something they keep waiting for.

The same thing can happen when someone is working on a project. They keep putting in effort, completing one task after another, and expecting a result. But sometimes the project takes much longer than expected, or the outcome is not what they imagined.

And then there is education. This is another situation where I think the play becomes particularly relatable.

You complete one degree, then another degree, and perhaps another one after that. You might pursue a master's degree, a doctorate, or spend years conducting research. You keep studying, submitting assignments, completing examinations, and working towards the next academic milestone.

But at some point, you might genuinely wonder: How long am I supposed to keep doing this? How many more examinations, qualifications, and applications do I need to complete?

Especially when your ultimate goal is simply to get a job and become financially independent, the process can sometimes feel endless. You keep adding qualifications because you believe that the next degree or the next achievement will finally bring the opportunity you want.

This is one reason some people prefer to enter the workforce earlier rather than spending many years collecting qualifications. There is nothing wrong with either choice. The point is that both paths can involve their own forms of waiting and uncertainty.

I think this is one of the strengths of Beckett's play. It does not describe only one person's struggle. It presents a situation that can be recognised in examinations, education, employment, health, relationships, and almost any other area where people continue working towards an uncertain outcome.

We keep waiting for the next opportunity, the next result, the next improvement, or the next stage of life. And sometimes, even after reaching one destination, we begin waiting for something else.

4. Who Is Godot? Hope, God, or Something We Cannot Identify?

One of the biggest questions in the play is also the simplest one: who exactly is Godot?

Vladimir and Estragon spend the entire play waiting for him, but we never actually see him. We do not receive a definite explanation of his identity, his purpose, or the reason he has kept them waiting.

This has led to several interpretations. Godot can be understood as a symbol of hope, salvation, God, a higher power, or some future event that people believe will finally provide meaning to their lives.

I personally find the uncertainty more interesting than any single interpretation. Godot could represent almost anything because people wait for different things depending on their circumstances.

For one person, Godot might be a job. For another, it might be financial stability. Someone else might be waiting for love, recognition, recovery, success, or simply a better phase of life.

The important thing is not necessarily what Godot represents. It is the fact that Vladimir and Estragon organise their time around someone whose arrival they cannot control.

They keep believing that something is going to happen, even though the play gives them very little reason to be certain about it.

And perhaps that is something we recognise in ourselves. We often attach our happiness to an expected future event, believing that once it happens, everything will finally make sense.

But what if it does not happen? And what if, even after it happens, we simply begin waiting for something else?

5. Vladimir and Estragon: Two Different People in the Same Situation

Vladimir and Estragon are the two central characters of the play. Although they are very different in their personalities, they are connected by the same situation: both are waiting for Godot.

Estragon is more concerned with immediate physical discomfort. He frequently complains about his feet and his shoes, struggles with discomfort, and appears more focused on the practical difficulties of everyday existence.

His worn-out shoes can also be connected with his difficult circumstances. He appears physically vulnerable and economically disadvantaged. His attention is often directed towards basic needs rather than abstract philosophical questions.

Vladimir, on the other hand, often appears more reflective and philosophical. He thinks about their situation, remembers things, questions what is happening, and tries to make sense of their circumstances.

We can almost see them as two different sides of human existence. Estragon is more closely connected with physical suffering and immediate needs, while Vladimir is more concerned with thought, memory, and philosophical reflection.

However, neither character is able to escape the situation they are in. Their differences do not change the fact that they are both waiting for the same uncertain arrival.

I find this particularly interesting because it shows that people can have very different personalities, levels of education, and ways of understanding life, yet still find themselves trapped in the same larger circumstances.

One person may try to understand the situation intellectually, while another may simply try to get through the day. But both are still waiting.

There is another way I think we can relate to these two characters. Imagine going for a job interview and finding yourself waiting outside with a group of other candidates. You might not know any of them. You might have completely different educational backgrounds, personalities, and career goals. But at that particular moment, you are all waiting for the same thing.

Perhaps you are waiting for your turn to enter the interview room. Perhaps you are waiting for the results of a group discussion, a hackathon, or some other selection process. You do not know exactly when your turn will come, what the outcome will be, or whether you will get the opportunity you are hoping for.

And somehow, in that situation, you begin feeling connected to the people around you. You might start talking to a complete stranger, sharing your experiences, asking about their preparation, or simply discussing how nervous you are. You may have nothing else in common, but the fact that you are all going through the same experience creates an immediate connection.

I think something similar happens with Vladimir and Estragon. They are very different individuals, but they are connected by the same expectation. Both are waiting for Godot. Their personalities may differ, and they may understand their circumstances differently, but they share the same uncertainty and the same hope that something will eventually happen.

And what makes this connection even more interesting is that they are not simply waiting for an event. They are waiting for the possibility of something happening. Just like those candidates outside an interview room, they do not know when their turn will come or what the final outcome will be. But the shared experience of waiting brings them together.

Sometimes, it is not even the thing we are waiting for that connects us to another person. It is the fact that we are waiting together, hoping for something, and experiencing the same uncertainty. I think this is one of the more human aspects of Vladimir and Estragon's relationship.

6. Pozzo and Lucky: Power, Slavery, and Submission

Apart from Vladimir and Estragon, the play introduces two other important characters: Pozzo and Lucky. Their relationship adds another interesting dimension to the play.

Pozzo is a powerful and controlling figure who treats Lucky as his servant. He keeps a rope around Lucky's neck and uses it to control his movements. Lucky is therefore presented as someone who has very little freedom within their relationship.

Lucky is almost entirely dependent on Pozzo. He carries Pozzo's belongings and follows his commands. Their relationship creates an image of domination and submission that can be interpreted in several ways.

At first, the relationship can be understood as a representation of slavery and unequal power. One person controls another, while the person being controlled is expected to obey.

We can also connect this relationship with wider social and economic structures. It can invite interpretations involving class inequality, exploitation, and capitalist systems in which some people exercise power over others.

What makes their relationship even more interesting is what happens in the second act. Pozzo returns blind, while Lucky has become mute. Their physical circumstances have changed, but Lucky continues to remain connected to Pozzo.

Even when Pozzo loses his sight and becomes dependent on assistance, Lucky does not simply walk away. The relationship continues, although its circumstances are now different.

This can be interpreted as a form of extreme submission or psychological dependence. Lucky appears to have become so accustomed to obeying Pozzo that his identity and actions seem almost inseparable from that relationship.

It also raises questions about how long-term domination can affect a person's sense of freedom. When someone spends their entire existence following another person's commands, they may gradually lose the ability to imagine a different way of living.

Some readers might connect this with ideas such as Stockholm syndrome, although the play does not establish that Lucky has this condition. I think it is more useful to understand their relationship broadly through power, dependence, conditioning, and submission.

For me, Pozzo and Lucky's relationship is important because it shows that the play is not concerned only with waiting. It also explores the unequal relationships and power structures that shape human existence.

7. The Circular Plot: Why Does Everything Keep Repeating?

One of the most noticeable features of Waiting for Godot is its circular structure. The two acts follow a remarkably similar pattern.

In both acts, Vladimir and Estragon meet at the same place, talk about their situation, consider leaving, and continue waiting for Godot. A boy arrives with a message that Godot will not come that day but may arrive the next day.

Even though certain details change, the larger situation remains almost exactly the same. The characters do not reach a meaningful conclusion, and their waiting continues.

This is why the play can feel repetitive. We might expect the second act to move the story forward and resolve the uncertainty created in the first act. Instead, it brings us back to a very similar situation.

I think this repetition is important because it reflects the experience of being stuck in a cycle. You keep doing something, hoping for a different outcome, but the overall situation does not seem to change.

It is similar to preparing for another examination, applying for another job, waiting for another result, or continuing a project that never seems to reach completion.

The repetition also challenges our expectations as readers and audiences. We are trained to expect a plot to move towards a resolution. Beckett deliberately refuses to give us that conventional satisfaction.

Instead of asking only what will happen next, we begin asking why these characters are still waiting and what this endless repetition might reveal about human life.

8. Existentialism: What If Life Does Not Give Us Clear Answers?

Another important aspect of Waiting for Godot is its connection with existential questions. The play explores human existence, uncertainty, isolation, and the difficulty of finding meaning.

We often expect life to follow a clear pattern. We study because we want a degree, get a degree because we want a job, get a job because we want stability, and continue moving towards whatever we believe will make our lives complete.

But real life does not always follow such a neat sequence. Sometimes we work hard without knowing whether our efforts will lead to the result we expect. Sometimes opportunities disappear, plans change, and answers never arrive.

Vladimir and Estragon seem to exist in precisely this kind of uncertainty. They are waiting for something that might give their situation a purpose, but they do not know when it will happen.

The play does not offer a simple philosophical answer to this problem. It does not tell us exactly what life means or how human beings should deal with uncertainty.

Instead, it presents characters who continue living, talking, arguing, and waiting despite their inability to understand their circumstances.

I think this is one of the reasons the play remains so relevant. We also spend a considerable amount of time trying to understand situations that do not always provide clear explanations.

9. Alienation: Are We Really Connected to Other People?

Another theme that I find particularly interesting is alienation. Although Vladimir and Estragon spend most of their time together, their conversations do not necessarily resolve their loneliness.

They talk constantly, but they often repeat themselves, misunderstand each other, argue, and struggle to communicate meaningfully. Their presence together does not completely remove their sense of isolation.

This is something I think we can connect with modern life as well. We have so many ways of communicating now. We use Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, LinkedIn, and several other platforms. We can send messages, share photographs, make calls, and remain connected to people throughout the day.

But does having access to all these platforms necessarily mean that we have meaningful relationships?

Sometimes we interact with dozens of people online but still feel lonely. We may have hundreds of contacts and followers, yet very few people with whom we can genuinely discuss what we are experiencing.

I am not saying that social media is necessarily bad or that online relationships cannot be meaningful. The point is that communication and genuine human connection are not always the same thing.

In a way, this makes Vladimir and Estragon's conversations feel surprisingly familiar. They keep talking because they are together, but their conversations do not necessarily provide the certainty or fulfilment they are looking for.

The play therefore raises an interesting question: can people be surrounded by others and still experience profound loneliness?

10. Hope and Despair: Why Do Vladimir and Estragon Keep Waiting?

If Godot never arrives, why do Vladimir and Estragon continue waiting for him?

This is perhaps one of the most important questions in the entire play. They know that Godot has not arrived, and they have already experienced the disappointment of being told that he will come another day.

Yet they continue to believe that he might arrive tomorrow.

I think this reflects the complicated relationship between hope and disappointment. Even when something does not happen today, we often convince ourselves that it might happen tomorrow.

Someone who has not received a job offer might continue applying. Someone who has not cleared an examination might prepare for another attempt. Someone who is waiting for a difficult situation to improve might continue hoping that the next day will be better.

Hope can help people continue moving forward. But it can also keep them attached to an expectation that may never be fulfilled.

I do not think the play gives us a straightforward answer about whether Vladimir and Estragon's waiting is an expression of hope or an inability to move beyond their circumstances.

Perhaps it is both. They continue waiting because they hope something will change, but their waiting also prevents them from doing anything different.

11. The Ending: They Decide to Leave, but They Do Not Move

The ending of Waiting for Godot is particularly interesting because it brings the play back to the same situation with which it began.

Vladimir and Estragon are still waiting for Godot. They have received another message that he will not come today but might arrive tomorrow.

They talk about leaving and moving on, but neither of them actually moves. Their words suggest a decision, but their actions do not follow.

This is such an effective ending because it does not provide the audience with the resolution they might have been expecting. Instead, it reinforces the circular nature of the play.

The characters remain exactly where they were, and the possibility of another day of waiting is still present.

I think this is also a very interesting representation of how people sometimes behave in real life. We may repeatedly say that we will change something, leave a situation, try a different approach, or stop waiting for a particular outcome.

But saying that we will do something and actually doing it are two very different things.

The ending leaves us with that uncomfortable feeling. The characters know that they are waiting, they talk about leaving, and yet they remain where they are.

And perhaps the most important thing is that the audience is left waiting too. We have spent the entire play expecting Godot to arrive, but he never does.

12. Is Waiting for Godot a Play, a Story, or Something Else?

Coming to its literary classification, Waiting for Godot is a play written by Samuel Beckett. More specifically, it is an absurdist drama associated with modernist literature and existential themes.

It is divided into two acts. Unlike conventional drama, however, it does not depend on a complicated sequence of events or a clearly developing plot.

Its dramatic structure is deliberately repetitive. The characters return to the same location, repeat similar conversations, and continue waiting for someone who never appears.

The play also makes extensive use of pauses, silences, physical comedy, repetition, misunderstandings, and apparently pointless conversations. These features contribute to its unusual theatrical experience.

It is important to understand that absurdist drama does not necessarily mean that every event must be completely random or that the characters must speak in meaningless sentences. The term refers to a broader dramatic approach that questions conventional ideas of meaning, order, and human existence.

In Waiting for Godot, the unusual structure is itself meaningful. The repetition, lack of resolution, and absence of Godot all contribute to the questions the play asks about life.

So, I would describe it as a modernist absurdist play in which the unconventional dramatic structure is central to its meaning.

💜 Final Thoughts

Waiting for Godot is certainly one of the most unusual plays I have come across. At first, it can feel confusing because there is no conventional storyline, no clear destination, and no satisfying resolution.

But once we begin thinking about the idea of waiting, the play becomes much easier to connect with everyday life.

The competitive-examination analogy is one of my favourite ways of understanding it. We prepare, appear for examinations, wait for results, face disappointments, and prepare again. Sometimes we keep repeating the same process because we believe that the next attempt might finally give us what we want.

The same idea can be applied to education, employment, health, relationships, and several other situations. We keep working towards something without always knowing when it will happen or whether it will happen at all.

I also find the contrast between Vladimir and Estragon interesting. They have different personalities and different ways of dealing with their circumstances, but they are ultimately caught in the same situation.

Pozzo and Lucky introduce another important dimension through their relationship of control and submission. Their story makes us think about power, dependence, exploitation, and the difficulty of escaping established relationships.

And then there is Godot himself. We never discover exactly who he is, and perhaps that is what makes him such an interesting figure. He can represent whatever it is that people are waiting for, hoping for, or expecting to arrive and change their lives.

What I particularly like about this play is that it does not give us easy answers. It makes us sit with uncertainty and think about why we continue waiting, even when we do not know whether the thing we are waiting for will ever arrive.

If you are interested in modernist literature, the Theatre of the Absurd, existentialism, and unconventional dramatic structures, I would definitely recommend reading Waiting for Godot.
