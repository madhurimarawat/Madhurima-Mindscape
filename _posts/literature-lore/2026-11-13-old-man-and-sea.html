The Old Man and the Sea By Ernest Hemingway

November 13, 2026

This is one of those books that makes you wonder how something written in such simple language can contain so many ideas. It is a relatively short novella, and the story itself is not particularly complicated. An old fisherman goes out to sea, catches an enormous marlin, struggles with it for days, and eventually returns with little more than its skeleton.

That sounds like a very straightforward fishing story, doesn't it? But it is so much more than that. It explores ageing, loneliness, friendship, perseverance, dignity, the relationship between human beings and nature, and the meaning of victory and defeat.

The Old Man and the Sea by Ernest Hemingway (Source)

What I particularly enjoy about Hemingway is his writing style. There are writers who use extraordinarily beautiful, ornate language, elaborate descriptions, and complicated metaphors to communicate their ideas. Hemingway seems to do almost the opposite. His sentences are often short, direct, and made up of everyday words. He writes about ordinary things in an almost conversational manner.

And yet, beneath that simplicity, there is an enormous amount of meaning. Sometimes a sentence appears to be describing nothing more than a fisherman looking at the sea, but when you think about it, you realise that it is communicating something much larger about life.

I also find it interesting that Santiago is not presented as some extraordinary philosophical figure who spends the entire novel discussing the meaning of existence. He thinks about fishing, baseball, food, his hands, the birds, the sea, and the boy. He even talks to himself and the fish. He feels hungry, tired, lonely, frustrated, and hopeful.

That is what makes him feel like a real human being. He is an ordinary old fisherman, but his struggle gradually becomes something much bigger than fishing.

And perhaps the most interesting thing about the novel is that even after losing almost everything he worked so hard to obtain, Santiago does not entirely lose his dignity.

A man can be destroyed but not defeated.

About the Author

Ernest Hemingway was an American novelist, short-story writer, and journalist, born on 21st July 1899 in Oak Park, Illinois. He is considered one of the most influential writers of twentieth-century American literature.

Hemingway is particularly known for his distinctive writing style, which is characterised by short sentences, simple vocabulary, restrained descriptions, and an avoidance of unnecessary ornamentation. His approach to storytelling was very different from that of many writers who preferred elaborate prose and lengthy descriptions.

He was also someone who travelled extensively and experienced several different parts of the world. His experiences included working as a journalist, participating in wartime reporting, living in Europe, spending time in Africa, and eventually becoming closely associated with Cuba.

His travels and personal experiences influenced many of his works. Fishing, hunting, war, physical endurance, masculinity, courage, and the relationship between human beings and nature appear repeatedly in his writing.

Some of his best-known works include The Sun Also Rises, A Farewell to Arms, For Whom the Bell Tolls, and The Old Man and the Sea.

The Old Man and the Sea was published in 1952 and became one of his most celebrated works. It received the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction in 1953 and contributed significantly to Hemingway receiving the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1954.

Hemingway's influence on modern prose is particularly significant because he demonstrated how much could be communicated without explicitly explaining everything. His famous iceberg theory suggests that a story can communicate far more than what is directly stated on the page. Much of its meaning remains beneath the surface.

A Quick Summary: What Is The Old Man and the Sea About?

The Old Man and the Sea follows Santiago, an elderly Cuban fisherman who has been experiencing an extraordinarily long period of bad luck. He has gone eighty-four days without catching a fish, and people in the village have begun to consider him unlucky.

A young boy named Manolin used to fish with him. However, after forty days without a catch, Manolin's parents force him to move to another boat. Interestingly, the boy still cares deeply about Santiago and continues helping him whenever he can.

On the eighty-fifth day, Santiago decides to go farther into the sea than usual. Eventually, a gigantic marlin takes his bait. The fish is so powerful that it begins pulling the boat out into the open sea.

Santiago spends several days struggling with the fish. His hands become injured, his body grows exhausted, and he has very little food. Yet he refuses to give up. During this struggle, he develops an extraordinary respect for the marlin, admiring its strength, dignity, and determination.

Eventually, he manages to kill the fish and ties it alongside his boat. Unfortunately, the marlin's blood attracts sharks. They begin attacking the enormous fish, and Santiago fights desperately to protect his catch.

Despite his efforts, the sharks gradually consume almost the entire marlin. By the time Santiago returns to the village, only its enormous skeleton remains attached to his boat.

The other fishermen are astonished by the size of the fish. Manolin is particularly distressed when he sees Santiago's injured hands and realises how much the old man has suffered.

At the end, Santiago returns to his shack and falls asleep. The boy sits beside him, watching over him, while the old man dreams once again of the lions he used to see on the beaches of Africa.

On the surface, it is a story about a fisherman who catches a magnificent fish but loses most of it to sharks. Beneath that, however, it is a story about human endurance, dignity, ageing, loneliness, and the determination to continue even when success seems impossible.

1. Hemingway's Writing Style: Simple Language, Enormous Meaning

One of the first things I noticed while reading this novel was Hemingway's writing style. It is remarkably simple. His sentences are generally straightforward, the vocabulary is accessible, and he does not spend unnecessary time decorating every description.

There are writers who might describe a sunset using several elaborate metaphors, comparing the sky to different shades of gold, crimson, and amber. Hemingway might simply tell you that the sun was setting.

But somehow, that simplicity works beautifully.

I think this is particularly interesting because the novel contains enormous metaphors and philosophical ideas despite its very ordinary language. The sea, the marlin, the sharks, the lions, and even Santiago's injured hands can be interpreted in several different ways.

The story almost feels like someone narrating an experience rather than a writer deliberately trying to impress you with beautiful language. At times, it even has the quality of a travel account or a personal adventure story.

We are simply following an old fisherman through his day, observing what he sees, what he thinks, what he eats, and what happens to him. But gradually, these ordinary observations begin to carry much deeper meanings.

This is closely connected to Hemingway's famous iceberg theory. The idea is that a writer does not need to explain every emotion, idea, or meaning directly. A large part of the story can remain beneath the surface, allowing readers to discover it for themselves.

I particularly appreciate this approach because it does not make the novel feel like a philosophical lecture. You are allowed to experience the story first and interpret its deeper meanings afterwards.

And honestly, that is quite an achievement. Writing simply without making the story feel simplistic is not easy.

2. Santiago and Manolin: Even the Names Have Interesting Meanings

Something else I found interesting is the names Hemingway chooses for his characters. They are ordinary Spanish names, which makes sense considering that the story is set in a Cuban fishing community. But some of them also carry interesting historical and religious associations.

Let's start with Santiago.

Santiago is the Spanish form associated with Saint James. Saint James the Greater was one of the apostles of Jesus and is traditionally associated with Spain, where he is venerated as its patron saint.

This gives the old fisherman's name an interesting biblical association. Santiago's struggle at sea can be interpreted through ideas of suffering, sacrifice, endurance, and faith. His physical journey becomes almost spiritual in its intensity.

I would not say that every detail of Santiago's character is a direct religious reference. However, the name certainly allows readers to explore those associations.

Then we have Manolin.

Manolin is an affectionate diminutive of Manuel, a Spanish name connected to Emmanuel, traditionally understood as meaning "God is with us". It is a rather lovely association considering the role Manolin plays in Santiago's life.

The boy is almost a source of comfort, companionship, and hope for the old man. Even when he is no longer allowed to fish with Santiago, he does not abandon him emotionally.

He brings him food, helps him prepare his fishing equipment, worries about his health, and genuinely wants to take care of him.

So, although these names are perfectly natural within the Spanish-speaking setting of the novel, they also allow for interesting symbolic readings. Hemingway manages to keep the characters grounded in an ordinary world while leaving room for much larger interpretations.

3. Santiago Is Seriously Unlucky: Eighty-Four Days Without a Fish!

Now, coming to Santiago himself, this poor man has been having an absolutely terrible time.

He has gone eighty-four days without catching a single fish. Imagine being a fisherman and not catching anything for almost three months. At that point, even people who are generally optimistic would probably start questioning their career choices!

The situation becomes particularly interesting because the villagers have started considering him unlucky. The word used in the novel is salao, which refers to an exceptionally severe form of bad luck.

But after forty days without a fish the boy's parents had told him that the old man was now definitely and finally salao, which is the worst form of unlucky, and the boy had gone at their orders in another boat which caught three good fish the first week.

I find this detail important because Santiago's bad luck is not simply a private disappointment. It has affected how other people perceive him. His reputation as a fisherman has suffered, and even the boy's parents no longer consider him a suitable person to fish with.

But Santiago does not seem particularly interested in accepting the identity that other people have assigned to him. He continues fishing. He continues preparing his equipment. And eventually, he decides to go farther out into the sea.

This is where the novel begins to explore the difference between how society judges someone and how that person understands their own worth.

Santiago may have been unsuccessful for eighty-four days, but he has not forgotten how to fish. His experience, knowledge, and confidence are still there.

I think there is something quite relatable about this. Sometimes people begin treating someone as incapable simply because that person has experienced a period of failure. But failure and inability are not necessarily the same thing.

Santiago's story reminds us that a person's abilities do not disappear simply because their circumstances have become unfavourable.

4. The Spirit Remains Unbroken: Santiago's Greatest Strength

One of my favourite descriptions of Santiago comes very early in the novel. Hemingway describes his physical appearance and then makes a particularly interesting observation about his eyes.

Everything about him was old except his eyes and they were the same color as the sea and were cheerful and undefeated.

I absolutely love this description.

Santiago is physically old. His body carries the marks of years spent working in the sun and at sea. His skin is weathered, his hands are scarred, and he is clearly not as physically strong as he once was.

But his eyes are different. They still possess something that age has not managed to take away.

I think the word undefeated is particularly important here. Hemingway does not describe him as someone who has never experienced defeat. In fact, Santiago has experienced plenty of it. He has been unsuccessful for weeks, he is growing old, and he has lost much of the social respect he once enjoyed.

Yet something inside him remains unchanged.

This is perhaps the central idea of the novel. Physical defeat and spiritual defeat are not necessarily the same thing.

Santiago may eventually lose the marlin, but the struggle reveals something about his character that cannot be measured by the fish he brings back to the shore.

5. Manolin and Santiago: A Relationship That Feels Like Father and Son

Honestly, one of the sweetest aspects of this novel is the relationship between Santiago and Manolin.

The boy is not Santiago's biological son, but their relationship has a very affectionate, almost father-and-son quality. They have spent years fishing together, and Manolin clearly looks up to the old man.

What I particularly like is that their affection is not expressed through dramatic declarations. It is shown through very ordinary acts of kindness.

For example, when Santiago returns to his shack and falls asleep, Manolin comes back and takes care of him.

When the boy came back the old man was asleep in the chair and the sun was down. The boy took the old army blanket off the bed and spread it over the back of the chair and over the old man's shoulders.

This is such a small moment, but I find it incredibly touching.

The boy notices that Santiago is sleeping and quietly covers him with a blanket. There is no grand speech about love or loyalty. He simply sees that the old man might be cold and takes care of him.

I also like the way Manolin continues calling him "old man". It is not presented as an insult. It feels affectionate, familiar, and almost like a nickname that belongs to their relationship.

"Sleep well old man."

There is something very comforting about their conversations. They talk about fishing, baseball, food, and the things they have experienced together. Their relationship feels natural rather than artificially sentimental.

What makes it even more meaningful is that Manolin's parents have forced him to fish with another fisherman because they believe Santiago is unlucky. But the boy still visits him, brings him food, and wants to return to fishing with him.

In other words, the boy does not judge Santiago by his recent success. He knows the old man's abilities and respects him regardless of whether he catches fish.

And towards the end, when Manolin sees Santiago's injured hands, his reaction is particularly emotional.

The boy saw that the old man was breathing and then he saw the old man's hands and he started to cry. He went out very quietly to go to bring some coffee and all the way down the road he was crying.

I think this is one of the most beautiful moments in the novella. Manolin's first instinct is to take care of Santiago. Even while crying, he quietly goes to get him coffee.

It almost feels like his caregiving instinct takes over. He does not waste time thinking about what to do. He simply wants to make sure the old man is alright.

Their relationship also introduces an important contrast to Santiago's loneliness. He spends much of the novel physically alone at sea, but he is not completely without human connection.

Manolin is one of the strongest reminders that even when life becomes difficult, companionship and affection can make an enormous difference.

6. Ageing, Time, and Santiago's Relationship with His Own Life

Another thing I found interesting is the way Hemingway explores ageing. Santiago is an old man, but he is not simply presented as someone who has reached the end of his usefulness.

He is still working, still fishing, still thinking about the future, and still interested in the world around him.

There is a particularly interesting line when Santiago thinks about why old men wake up so early.

"Age is my alarm clock," the old man said. "Why do old men wake so early? Is it to have one longer day?"

I really like this observation because it is humorous, but there is also something rather sad about it.

The question suggests that Santiago is conscious of his age and the passage of time. Perhaps old people wake early because they have become accustomed to it. Perhaps they simply have fewer distractions. Or perhaps, as the line jokingly suggests, they want to make the most of whatever time they have.

Hemingway does not turn this into a lengthy philosophical discussion. He gives us one simple observation and allows us to think about it.

Santiago also remembers his younger days, particularly his experiences in Africa. His dreams of the lions on the beaches become a recurring connection to his youth.

I think these dreams are important because they allow us to see that although Santiago is physically old, his inner world is still connected to the person he once was.

He has not completely lost the memories, energy, and sense of wonder associated with his younger life.

The novel therefore presents ageing as something more complicated than simply becoming physically weaker. A person may grow old without losing their curiosity, memories, determination, or desire to live.

7. Africa and the Lions: Memories of Youth, Strength, and Freedom

One recurring image in the novel is Santiago's dream of the lions on the beaches of Africa.

He remembers seeing the African coast when he was young, particularly the beautiful beaches and the lions playing on the shore.

He was asleep in a short time and he dreamed of Africa when he was a boy and the long golden beaches and the white beaches...

I find this detail particularly interesting because the lions have very little to do with the immediate events of the fishing expedition. Yet Hemingway keeps bringing them back into the story.

Hemingway himself travelled extensively and spent time in Africa, including going on safaris. His own experiences of the continent provide an interesting background to these recurring memories.

But within the novel, the lions can also be interpreted symbolically. They represent youth, strength, vitality, and the freedom Santiago associates with his younger days.

I particularly like the fact that Santiago does not dream about his present struggles when he sleeps. He returns to the memories of his youth.

It is almost as though those memories give him a kind of emotional comfort. His body may be exhausted, but his imagination can still return to a time when he was young and full of energy.

The lions also create an interesting contrast with the sharks. Both are animals, but they carry very different associations within the story. The lions are connected to Santiago's peaceful memories, while the sharks become the destructive force that takes away his hard-earned catch.

I would not reduce the lions to a single fixed meaning. Their recurring appearance is effective precisely because they can represent several things at once: memory, youth, hope, strength, and an inner world that remains alive despite physical ageing.

8. The Sea: La Mar, Almost Like a Living Woman

One of my favourite aspects of the novel is the way Santiago thinks about the sea.

He does not treat it merely as a geographical location or a place where fishermen go to earn money. He has a deeply personal relationship with it.

Hemingway makes an interesting distinction between the way different fishermen refer to the sea.

He always thought of the sea as la mar which is what people call her in Spanish when they love her. Sometimes those who love her say bad things of her but they are always said as though she were a woman.

This is such a beautiful passage.

Santiago thinks of the sea as la mar, the feminine form in Spanish. He regards it almost as a woman who can be generous, unpredictable, affectionate, and sometimes cruel.

The younger fishermen, on the other hand, often refer to the sea as el mar, the masculine form. They tend to see it as something to compete against, something that provides opportunities for earning money, or even something that behaves like an enemy.

The difference is quite interesting because it reflects two different attitudes towards nature.

Santiago does not necessarily believe that the sea is always kind. He knows that it can be dangerous and unpredictable. But he accepts its nature and does not automatically treat every difficulty as a personal attack.

There is something almost affectionate about his relationship with the sea. He understands that it can give and take away, and he has learned to live with that uncertainty.

I think this also reflects Santiago's larger attitude towards life. He understands that the world is not always fair, but he does not allow that understanding to destroy his relationship with it.

The sea can be beautiful and dangerous at the same time. It can provide food and livelihood, but it can also take away everything. Santiago respects both sides of it.

9. Santiago Is Surprisingly Ordinary: Baseball, Fish, Birds, and Everyday Thoughts

Something I particularly enjoy about Santiago is that he is not constantly thinking about grand philosophical questions.

He is a fisherman. He thinks about fishing, the weather, his equipment, his food, baseball, and the things he needs to do to survive.

Sometimes he thinks about the boy. Sometimes he remembers his younger days. Sometimes he simply wishes he had someone with him.

There is a moment when he thinks:

No one should be alone in their old age, he thought. But it is unavoidable.

I find this particularly sad because it captures Santiago's loneliness without making a big emotional performance out of it.

He knows that having someone beside him would make things easier. He misses Manolin and wishes the boy were there to help him. But he also understands that he has to face the situation on his own.

What I find interesting is how naturally Hemingway moves between these different kinds of thoughts. Santiago can be thinking about the enormous fish one moment and baseball the next.

He is almost an expert at switching contexts!

And that is what makes his character feel so realistic. Real people do not spend every waking moment thinking about the biggest problem in their lives. Even when something serious is happening, their minds wander towards ordinary things.

Santiago also talks to himself frequently. He talks to the fish, the birds, and sometimes even wishes he had the boy with him so that he would not have to think aloud.

I think this is one of the most effective ways Hemingway communicates loneliness. Santiago is surrounded by the sea, but there is nobody with whom he can share his experiences.

So he creates conversations wherever he can.

There is something rather human about that. When people spend too much time alone, they sometimes begin talking to themselves, animals, or even inanimate objects. It is a way of maintaining some sense of companionship.

Santiago's conversations with the natural world make his loneliness feel less abstract and much more personal.

10. The Marlin: Respecting Something You Are Also Trying to Destroy

Now, this is one of the most interesting and complicated relationships in the novel.

Santiago catches an enormous marlin, but the longer he struggles with it, the more he begins to admire the fish.

He recognises its strength, intelligence, endurance, and beauty. He does not simply see it as something that needs to be captured. He sees it as a worthy opponent.

There is a particularly interesting passage where Santiago feels sorry for the fish because it has nothing to eat.

Then he was sorry for the great fish that had nothing to eat and his determination to kill him never relaxed in his sorrow for him. How many people will he feed, he thought. But are they worthy to eat him? No, of course not. There is no one worthy of eating him from the manner of his behaviour and his great dignity.

I find this passage fascinating because Santiago is simultaneously determined to kill the fish and deeply sympathetic towards it.

He admires the marlin so much that he almost considers it his equal. The fish is not simply prey. It is a magnificent creature that has demonstrated extraordinary strength.

This creates a rather complicated relationship between the fisherman and his catch.

Santiago respects the fish, but he also needs to kill it. Fishing is his livelihood, and he has spent his entire life understanding the relationship between survival and killing.

He cannot simply stop being a fisherman because he has developed affection for one particular fish.

And this is where the novel becomes particularly interesting from an environmental and philosophical perspective. Human beings are part of nature, but they also exploit it for survival. The relationship is not always peaceful or uncomplicated.

Santiago loves the natural world, but he is also capable of killing the creatures within it. He respects the marlin, but he cannot allow it to escape.

That contradiction is one of the most interesting aspects of his character.

11. The Male and Female Marlin: A Surprisingly Heartbreaking Incident

There is another incident in the novel that I found particularly interesting. While thinking about the great marlin he is currently trying to catch, Santiago remembers an earlier experience involving a pair of marlins.

On that occasion, he and the boy had managed to catch the female marlin. However, what makes the incident memorable is the behaviour of the male fish. It does not simply swim away after its companion gets caught. Instead, it stays beside her, following the boat and remaining close even as Santiago and the boy struggle to bring her aboard.

Hemingway describes the male fish jumping out of the water beside the boat, apparently trying to see where the female has gone. It eventually disappears into the depths of the sea.

Santiago remembers the male as a beautiful fish. The behaviour appears to him almost like an expression of loyalty or attachment. Of course, we cannot know exactly what the fish is thinking, but the scene is written in a way that makes it difficult not to feel something for the pair.

I find this entire incident surprisingly heartbreaking. Think about it. The male fish stays with the female even when she is caught and eventually killed. It does not immediately abandon her. And Santiago remembers this incident as something deeply sad.

What I particularly like is that Santiago and the boy are both affected by what they have witnessed. They feel sorry for the fish, beg her pardon, and then proceed to butcher her promptly. There is something rather interesting about that combination of compassion and practicality.

Santiago is a fisherman. Catching fish is how he earns his living. He cannot simply stop doing his work because he feels emotionally connected to the creatures he catches. Yet he is also capable of recognising their beauty, dignity, and behaviour.

This incident makes his relationship with the great marlin particularly interesting. As the struggle continues, Santiago begins to feel an extraordinary connection with the fish. He admires its strength, endurance, and determination. He even says:

"Fish," he said, "I love you and respect you very much. But I will kill you dead before this day ends."

Now, that is an interesting statement! He loves and respects the fish, but he is also determined to kill it. These two feelings exist together without cancelling each other out.

In a way, this is what makes Santiago's relationship with the marlin so complicated. He is not fighting an enemy he hates. He is fighting a creature he genuinely admires. He recognises its strength and considers it worthy of the struggle.

I also think the earlier incident involving the male and female marlin prepares us to understand this emotional connection. Santiago knows that these creatures are not simply objects to be caught. They have their own lives, and their behaviour can move him in ways he does not necessarily expect.

There is something almost contradictory about his attitude, but that is precisely what makes it realistic. He can admire something, feel affection for it, and still participate in its destruction because that is the nature of his livelihood.

And perhaps this is one of the more interesting questions Hemingway raises through Santiago: can you genuinely love and respect something while also being responsible for its death? Santiago himself seems to struggle with that question throughout his journey.

12. Fishing, Sin, and the Contradictions of Being Human

The more Santiago thinks about the marlin, the more complicated his feelings become. He knows that he is going to kill the fish, but he also wonders whether doing so is wrong.

This is particularly interesting because Santiago is not someone who has suddenly discovered that fishing involves killing. He has been a fisherman all his life. He knows exactly what his profession requires.

Yet the marlin is different. It is so magnificent that Santiago begins to question the very act he has performed countless times before.

Do not think about sin. It is much too late for that and there are people who are paid to do it. Let them think about it. You were born to be a fisherman as the fish was born to be a fish.

I find this passage fascinating because Santiago tries to justify what he is doing by reminding himself that fishing is his nature. He was born to be a fisherman, just as the fish was born to be a fish.

But he does not completely settle the question. He continues thinking about whether loving the fish makes killing it a sin.

You loved him when he was alive and you loved him after. If you love him, it is not a sin to kill him. Or is it more?

This is one of those moments where Santiago's internal conversation becomes almost philosophical. He is trying to make sense of two feelings that appear to contradict each other.

I think the novel is quite honest about this contradiction. People often have relationships with the world that are not entirely consistent. We can appreciate nature and still use it. We can admire something and still compete against it. We can care about another living being while recognising that our own survival may depend on it.

Santiago does not have a neat answer to these questions. And perhaps that is the point. Hemingway allows him to think through the problem without forcing a final moral conclusion on the reader.

I remember watching a wildlife documentary where someone made an observation that stayed with me: the first animal you see is often the one you end up sympathising with. And I think that's true. If I first see a hungry predator struggling to find food, I find myself hoping it catches something. I understand its hunger, and I want it to survive. But if the documentary first introduces me to a small, innocent-looking animal peacefully grazing, I suddenly find myself hoping it escapes. When the predator finally catches it, I feel sorry for the prey, even though I know the predator was only trying to feed itself.

It's strange how easily our sympathy changes depending on which side of the story we see first. For a moment, one animal becomes the hero and the other the villain, and then, with a change in perspective, those roles reverse. Perhaps that's what makes Santiago's relationship with the marlin so interesting to me. We want Santiago to succeed because we have followed his struggle and understand how much this catch means to him.

But have we ever stopped to consider the marlin's side? From its perspective, Santiago is simply a threat trying to take its life. It isn't fighting to defeat a man or prove its strength; it is fighting because it wants to survive, just as Santiago does. And when I try to look at the struggle from both sides, I find myself rooting for Santiago while also hoping, somewhere in the back of my mind, that the marlin might escape. Perhaps neither of them is the villain here. They are simply two living beings caught in a struggle where one has to lose for the other to survive.

13. The Marlin as a Symbol of Ambition and the Worthy Challenge

The great marlin can also be interpreted as something larger than a fish. It becomes a representation of the difficult goals people pursue throughout their lives.

Santiago has spent eighty-four days without catching anything. When he finally hooks the marlin, he is presented with the greatest challenge of his fishing career. The fish is enormous, powerful, and incredibly difficult to bring in.

In a way, the marlin represents the kind of achievement that demands everything a person has.

Santiago could have caught a smaller fish and returned home. But this is not an ordinary catch. It is something that tests his experience, patience, strength, and determination.

I think this is why he develops so much respect for it. The value of the challenge is connected to how difficult it is to overcome.

There is a broader human idea here. Sometimes the things we want most are also the things that demand the greatest sacrifices. The effort involved can become just as significant as the eventual result.

The marlin is therefore not simply an obstacle in Santiago's way. It is also what gives his journey its meaning.

14. The Sharks: When Life Takes Away What You Have Worked For

And then, just when Santiago has finally succeeded in killing the marlin, the sharks arrive.

This is perhaps the most frustrating part of the entire story. After spending days struggling with the fish, injuring his hands, exhausting himself, and pushing his body to its limits, Santiago finally manages to catch it. But he cannot enjoy his achievement for very long.

The blood of the marlin attracts sharks, and they begin attacking the fish tied alongside his boat.

Santiago fights them with everything he has. He uses his harpoon, his knife, and eventually whatever equipment he can manage to use as a weapon. But the sharks keep coming.

I think the sharks can be interpreted as the destructive forces that sometimes threaten human achievements. A person can spend years building something, only to see circumstances beyond their control gradually destroy it.

The sharks are not necessarily evil in a human sense. They are acting according to their nature, just as Santiago is acting according to his. But from Santiago's perspective, they represent the loss of something he has worked incredibly hard to obtain.

There is also an interesting contrast between the marlin and the sharks. Santiago sees the marlin as a worthy opponent. He admires its strength and dignity. The sharks, however, are associated with destruction and the gradual consumption of his achievement.

I would be careful about treating them as a simple symbol of evil, because Hemingway's natural world is more complicated than that. The sharks are animals too, following their instincts. Their destructive role in the story does not necessarily make them villains.

And that is what makes the situation particularly painful. Santiago cannot even hate them in a straightforward way. He understands that this is part of the natural world, but he still fights them because the marlin matters to him.

Eventually, almost the entire fish is eaten. Santiago returns with little more than its skeleton.

His physical catch has been destroyed, but the struggle itself has not become meaningless.

15. Going Too Far Out: Ambition, Regret, and the Limits of Human Control

There is another interesting aspect of Santiago's struggle that I think deserves attention. At a certain point, he begins wondering whether he has gone too far out into the sea.

He has caught an extraordinary fish, but he is also increasingly isolated. He is far away from the shore, physically exhausted, and unable to protect his catch from the sharks.

As the sharks attack the marlin, Santiago begins regretting the decision that brought him so far out.

"I wish it were a dream and that I had never hooked him. I'm sorry about it, fish. It makes everything wrong."

He also says:

"I shouldn't have gone out so far, fish," he said. "Neither for you nor for me. I'm sorry, fish."

I find these moments particularly moving because Santiago is not simply regretting his own suffering. He is also apologising to the fish.

He has begun to feel that the struggle has become wrong in some way, not because he has stopped respecting the marlin, but because the consequences of his decision have become so destructive.

This can also be interpreted as a reflection on ambition. Sometimes we pursue something so intensely that we fail to consider the consequences of going too far. We become so focused on achieving the goal that we forget to think about what the pursuit might cost.

I do not think the novel simply tells us that ambition is bad. Santiago's determination is one of his greatest qualities. But his journey also reminds us that determination does not give us complete control over the outcome.

We can make decisions, work hard, and prepare ourselves as well as possible. But there are still things that remain outside our control.

16. The Physical Struggle: Pain, Endurance, and the Human Body

One thing Hemingway does particularly well is describe Santiago's physical suffering without making it unnecessarily dramatic.

His hands cramp, his palms are cut, his back aches, and his body becomes increasingly exhausted. He has to manage hunger, thirst, sleep deprivation, and the constant strain of holding the fishing line.

Yet he continues.

What I find interesting is that Santiago's determination is not presented as something that makes him immune to pain. He suffers throughout the journey. He becomes frustrated, tired, and frightened. He even has moments when he doubts whether he can continue.

His courage is not the absence of weakness. It is his willingness to keep going despite being weak.

This is an important distinction because stories about heroic characters sometimes make endurance seem effortless. Santiago's struggle feels much more human. He has to persuade himself to continue, remind himself of his experience, and find strength wherever he can.

His body may be failing him, but his mind continues searching for a way forward.

The novel therefore presents endurance not as a magical quality that some people possess, but as something that is demonstrated through repeated decisions to keep trying.

17. Santiago's Suffering: Possible Biblical and Christ-Like Associations

Considering the religious associations of Santiago's name, it is interesting to look at some of the physical imagery surrounding his journey.

Towards the end of the novella, Santiago carries the mast of his boat up the hill towards his shack. He is exhausted, injured, and struggling to make his way home. The image can remind readers of Christ carrying the cross.

His body is bent under the weight, and the journey home becomes another expression of his suffering.

I think this is a particularly interesting interpretation because Santiago's struggle has already taken on something almost spiritual. He has endured pain, isolation, hunger, and loss, but he continues moving forward.

His journey is not literally the same as the Passion of Christ, and I would not insist that every detail is intended as a direct biblical parallel. But the imagery certainly allows for that comparison.

The connection also makes the ending more meaningful. Santiago returns physically defeated, carrying the remains of his catch and the burden of his suffering. Yet his dignity remains intact.

His struggle can therefore be read as a kind of spiritual endurance: the ability to continue bearing hardship without allowing it to destroy one's sense of self.

18. The Skeleton of the Marlin: What Does Victory Actually Look Like?

When Santiago finally returns to the village, the marlin has been reduced to a skeleton. The fish that once represented his greatest achievement is now little more than its enormous bones and tail.

At first glance, this seems like a complete failure. Santiago spent days trying to catch the fish, only to lose almost all of it. He cannot sell the meat or bring home the full catch he had imagined.

But there is something interesting about the way the other fishermen react when they see the skeleton. They are astonished by its size. Even though the fish has been eaten, its remains reveal just how magnificent it was.

This makes me think about the difference between the visible outcome of an effort and the actual value of the effort itself.

Santiago has lost the physical reward, but he has demonstrated extraordinary skill, courage, and perseverance. The skeleton becomes evidence of what he accomplished, even if he could not preserve the entire fish.

It can also be interpreted as a reminder that material achievements are temporary. Something we work hard to obtain can disappear, deteriorate, or be taken away. But the experience of pursuing it can still reveal something important about who we are.

I think this is one reason the novel's title remains so interesting. It is not called The Great Marlin. It is called The Old Man and the Sea. The focus is ultimately on Santiago and his relationship with the world around him, not simply on the fish he catches.

19. The Ending: A Quiet Return to Life

One of the things I appreciate most about the ending is how understated it is.

After everything Santiago has endured, the novel does not end with a grand celebration or a dramatic speech about his victory. He returns home, exhausted, and falls asleep.

Manolin comes to see him and sits beside him. He is relieved that Santiago is alive, but he is also deeply affected by the old man's injuries.

The boy wants to take care of him, and he also expresses his determination to fish with Santiago again.

I find this particularly comforting because it suggests that the story does not end with Santiago's defeat. There is still a relationship waiting for him when he returns. There is still someone who cares about him.

And then Hemingway gives us one final image:

Up the road, in his shack, the old man was sleeping again. He was still sleeping on his face and the boy was sitting by him watching him. The old man was dreaming about the lions.

I absolutely love this ending.

After all the pain, struggle, and loss, Santiago returns to the same dream that has accompanied him throughout the novella. The lions are still there. His memories of youth and vitality remain.

The ending also brings us back to Manolin. The boy is watching over him, just as he has done before. It is almost as though life has quietly returned to its ordinary rhythm.

The old man sleeps. The boy sits beside him. And the world continues.

There is something beautiful about that lack of drama. Hemingway does not need to tell us that Santiago will recover or that everything will be alright. He simply leaves us with an image of companionship and a dream.

20. So, Was Santiago Defeated or Did He Actually Win?

This is probably the biggest question I was left thinking about after finishing the novella.

If we look at the story purely in practical terms, Santiago has not achieved what he wanted. He goes out to catch a fish, spends days fighting it, finally kills it, and then loses almost the entire catch to sharks.

He returns without the fish he had hoped to bring home. His hands are injured, his body is exhausted, and he has gained very little materially from the entire experience.

But does that mean he has been defeated?

I do not think the novel gives us such a simple answer.

Santiago's physical achievement has been taken away, but the struggle has demonstrated the strength of his character. He has faced something extraordinarily difficult and continued fighting even when the situation became almost impossible.

The famous line about a man being destroyed but not defeated captures this distinction rather beautifully.

Being destroyed can mean losing something external: a possession, an achievement, physical strength, or even the results of years of work. Being defeated, in a deeper sense, might mean allowing that loss to take away your sense of worth and purpose.

Santiago loses the marlin, but he does not lose the qualities that made him capable of catching it in the first place.

He is still the fisherman who went out into the sea. He still has his experience, his memories, his dignity, and his connection with Manolin.

Perhaps that is the real victory of the novel. Not that Santiago manages to keep everything he has worked for, but that losing it does not erase what he has demonstrated.

21. The Sea as a Metaphor for Life

Looking at the novel as a whole, I think the sea itself can be interpreted as a metaphor for life.

It is vast, unpredictable, beautiful, and sometimes dangerous. It provides opportunities, but it also creates difficulties. There are things we can understand about it through experience, but there will always be things beyond our control.

Santiago knows the sea extremely well. He understands its movements, the behaviour of fish, the weather, and the different signs that help him navigate his work. Yet even his experience cannot guarantee a successful outcome.

This feels rather similar to life. We learn, prepare, make decisions, and develop skills. But there are still unexpected events that can change everything.

I also like the fact that Santiago does not seem to resent the sea simply because it is unpredictable. He loves it, respects it, and continues returning to it.

Perhaps this is one of the most meaningful ideas in the book: accepting that life can be difficult without losing our willingness to participate in it.

22. Final Thoughts: A Simple Story That Stays With You

I think what makes The Old Man and the Sea such a memorable novella is that it does not try to explain everything to the reader.

Hemingway gives us an old fisherman, a boy who cares about him, a vast sea, and a magnificent fish. He describes their actions in simple language and allows the larger questions to emerge naturally from the story.

We begin by following Santiago's fishing expedition, but gradually find ourselves thinking about ageing, loneliness, ambition, dignity, nature, faith, and the meaning of success.

I particularly appreciate that Santiago is not portrayed as a flawless hero. He is stubborn, lonely, sometimes doubtful, and occasionally makes decisions that he later regrets. He talks to himself, misses the boy, worries about his body, and tries to make sense of his own actions.

In other words, he is human.

And perhaps that is why his struggle is so relatable. Most of us will never spend three days fighting a giant marlin in the middle of the sea. But we do understand what it means to work hard for something, to face setbacks, and to discover that the final outcome is not always within our control.

The novel does not promise that perseverance will always lead to material success. Santiago's experience makes that very clear. But it does suggest that the way we face difficulties matters.

Sometimes we lose what we have worked for. Sometimes our efforts do not produce the results we expected. Sometimes circumstances are simply beyond our control.

But those things do not necessarily determine our worth.

And that, to me, is what makes this seemingly simple story so powerful. It is not really about catching a fish. It is about what a human being is capable of enduring, and what remains of us when the things we value are taken away.

Santiago returns with only the skeleton of the marlin. But he also returns with his dignity, his memories, and the boy who still believes in him.

And perhaps that is enough.
