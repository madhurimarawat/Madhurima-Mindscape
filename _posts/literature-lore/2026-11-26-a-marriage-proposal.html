A Marriage Proposal by Anton Chekhov

November 26, 2026

Okay, this play is absolutely hilarious! 😂 Imagine going to someone's house to propose marriage, only to spend most of your visit arguing with the girl about ancestral property, family history, and whose dog is better. And somehow, after all that drama, you still end up getting engaged!

That is essentially what happens in Anton Chekhov's A Marriage Proposal. It is a short one-act comedy, but the amount of unnecessary drama packed into it is impressive.

We have Lomov, a nervous gentleman who visits his neighbour, Choobookov, because he wants to propose to his daughter, Natalia Stepanovna. Choobookov is delighted when he discovers the purpose of the visit. Natalia is also apparently quite marriageable, and everything should ideally proceed smoothly.

Except it doesn't.

Before Lomov can even properly propose, he and Natalia start arguing about a piece of land called the Oxen Meadows. Then they move on to insulting each other's families, bringing up old scandals, and questioning each other's intelligence and character.

And just when you think things cannot get any more ridiculous, they start arguing about their dogs.

I genuinely think someone needs to translate this play into Hindi and give it the full Indian television serial treatment. Imagine the dramatic background music, zoom-in shots, shocked expressions, relatives appearing out of nowhere, and someone shouting, "Yeh zameen hamari hai!"

Because honestly, the entire play feels like an Indian family gathering where two people are supposed to get married, but everyone has somehow found a reason to fight.

And the funniest part? They actually do get married. Well, almost. 😂

About the Author

Anton Chekhov was a Russian playwright and short-story writer, born on January 29, 1860, in Taganrog, Russia. He is considered one of the most influential figures in modern drama and short fiction.

Chekhov was particularly skilled at portraying ordinary people, their everyday conversations, personal insecurities, relationships, and ridiculous little conflicts. His works often explore human behaviour through humour, irony, and subtle observations.

Some of his best-known plays include The Seagull, Uncle Vanya, Three Sisters, and The Cherry Orchard.

A Marriage Proposal, also known as The Proposal, is a one-act farce first performed in 1890. It makes fun of social conventions, marriage negotiations, personal pride, and the tendency of people to turn even the smallest disagreements into enormous conflicts.

Meet the Characters

Before getting into all the arguments, property disputes, and family drama, let's meet the three people responsible for this absolute chaos.

1. Stepan Stepanovitch Choobookov

Choobookov is a landowner and the father of Natalia Stepanovna. He is Lomov's neighbour and the person whose house becomes the setting for most of the drama.

Initially, he is delighted when Lomov visits because he assumes something important is happening. Once he discovers that Lomov wants to marry his daughter, he becomes extremely enthusiastic about the proposal.

But Choobookov is not exactly a peaceful person himself. He quickly joins the arguments, insults Lomov's family, and contributes to the shouting match. By the end, he is so exhausted by the two of them that he practically forces them to get married.

Honestly, he just wants some peace and quiet. 😂

2. Natalia Stepanovna

Natalia is Choobookov's twenty-five-year-old daughter. She is a landowner's daughter, confident, strong-willed, and extremely stubborn.

When Lomov visits, she has no idea that he has come to propose marriage. Instead of having a romantic conversation with him, she immediately gets involved in an argument about the ownership of the Oxen Meadows.

She is just as argumentative as Lomov and refuses to accept that he might be right. She also gets involved in insulting his family and later argues with him about whose dog is better.

However, when she discovers that Lomov actually came to ask for her hand in marriage, her attitude changes completely. Suddenly, she desperately wants him brought back.

A woman who can go from threatening someone with a court case to asking them to marry her in a matter of minutes. What a character! 😂

3. Ivan Vassilievitch Lomov

Lomov is a twenty-five-year-old neighbour of the Choobookov family and a landowner. He is described as healthy and well-fed, but terribly hypochondriacal, meaning he is constantly worried about his health and believes he is suffering from various illnesses.

He visits Choobookov's house wearing formal clothes because he has decided to propose marriage to Natalia. Unfortunately, he is extremely nervous and struggles to get to the point.

Lomov is also incredibly sensitive and stubborn. He gets worked up over the smallest disagreements, whether they concern land ownership or the qualities of his dog, Guess.

His health complaints make the arguments even funnier because he repeatedly feels as though he is about to collapse.

Poor man comes to propose and nearly needs a doctor before he can finish the conversation. 😂

And that's our little cast! Three landowners, one marriage proposal, two major arguments, several family insults, and absolutely no shortage of drama.

A Quick Summary: What Is A Marriage Proposal About?

The play revolves around Ivan Vassilievitch Lomov, a nervous and somewhat hypochondriacal landowner who visits his neighbour, Stepan Stepanovitch Choobookov.

He has come with an important intention: he wants to ask Choobookov's daughter, Natalia Stepanovna, to marry him.

Choobookov is initially surprised but becomes extremely happy when he understands the reason behind the visit. He welcomes the proposal enthusiastically.

However, when Natalia joins Lomov, the two somehow begin arguing about the ownership of a piece of land called the Oxen Meadows.

What should have been a romantic conversation turns into a heated property dispute. They both insist that the land belongs to their respective families, and neither is willing to back down.

Choobookov eventually joins the argument, and the situation becomes even more chaotic. Old family insults are brought up, relatives are discussed, and everyone starts shouting.

Lomov eventually leaves in a terrible physical and emotional state.

But then Choobookov reveals to Natalia that Lomov had actually come to propose marriage.

Suddenly, Natalia changes her attitude completely. She starts crying and desperately asks her father to bring Lomov back.

Lomov returns, and just when things seem to be getting somewhere, they begin arguing again. This time, the subject is their dogs: Guess and Squeezer.

The argument becomes so intense that Lomov nearly collapses. Choobookov panics, thinking he is dying, and desperately tries to revive him.

Eventually, Choobookov joins their hands, announces that Natalia accepts the proposal, and tells them to get married.

And what happens next?

They continue arguing about the dogs.

Because apparently, getting engaged does not mean you have to stop fighting. 😂

1. Lomov Arrives to Propose: A Nervous Man with a Very Important Mission

So, Lomov arrives at Choobookov's house dressed in formal clothes, clearly nervous about what he is about to do.

He has made up his mind to propose to Natalia, but he seems almost incapable of getting the words out properly.

LOMOV: (nervously): You see, my esteemed Natalia Stepanovna..... the fact is I've made up my mind to ask you to listen to me. Naturally you'll be surprised and even angry, but I...

And then, in the middle of trying to express his feelings, he suddenly thinks about how cold it is!

LOMOV: (aside): God, it's cold!

I mean, dude! You have come to propose marriage. Can we please focus on the actual mission? 😂

He then tries to explain how long his family has known Natalia's family and how close their families have always been.

And this is where things start becoming interesting.

Instead of talking about love or compatibility, Lomov begins explaining the history of their families, their estates, and their neighbouring properties.

Honestly, this is already sounding less like a romantic proposal and more like a property registration meeting.

He is basically establishing that their families have known each other for years, their properties are adjacent, and everything is perfectly suitable for a marriage.

In an Indian context, I can almost imagine someone saying, "Beta, dono families ek doosre ko bahut achhe se jaanti hain, properties bhi paas-paas hain. Rishta toh bilkul perfect hai!"

And then Natalia interrupts him.

2. The First Argument: Excuse Me, Whose Land Is This?

Lomov is trying to explain how his family's property borders Natalia's property when he casually mentions his Oxen Meadows.

NATALIA: Excuse me for interrupting you. You said "my Oxen Meadows". Are they yours?

And there we go.

The proposal has officially been postponed. We now have a property dispute to settle.

Lomov explains that the land belongs to him, while Natalia insists that it belongs to her family.

Both begin presenting their arguments with complete confidence. They bring up old documents, family ownership, historical claims, and what their respective relatives supposedly knew about the land.

I find this particularly funny because Lomov has come to ask for Natalia's hand in marriage, and within minutes they are behaving like two neighbours fighting over a boundary wall.

Imagine this happening in an Indian village.

Someone comes with a marriage proposal, and the girl's family suddenly says, "Shaadi ki baat baad mein karenge. Pehle yeh batao, jo khet ke peeche wali zameen hai, woh tumhari hai ya hamari?"

Then someone brings out an old property document, another person calls an uncle for confirmation, and suddenly the entire village has gathered.

And the funniest thing is that neither Lomov nor Natalia seems interested in letting the matter go.

They are already arguing like an old married couple. At this point, they might as well get married and save everyone the trouble!

3. Family Gossip: When the Entire Ancestral History Comes Out

Now, the property argument is already ridiculous, but Chekhov takes things to another level.

Instead of limiting themselves to the actual disagreement, the characters begin attacking each other's families.

And this is where the play starts feeling like an Indian family drama.

You know how one small disagreement at a family gathering can suddenly turn into a discussion about something someone's grandfather did forty years ago?

That is exactly what happens here.

CHOOBOOKOV: Your grandfather drank like a fish, and the whole country knows that your youngest aunt, Nastasia, ran off with an architect, and what not —

Excuse me, WHAT? 😂

We have officially moved from property ownership to family gossip.

Lomov does not stay quiet either.

LOMOV: And your mother was a hunchback! (clutching at his heart): There's a twitching in my side. My head's throbbing.... Oh, God! Water!

And Choobookov responds by insulting his father.

CHOOBOOKOV: And your father was a gambler and he ate like a pig!

Natalia joins in too.

NATALIA: And no one could beat your aunt at scandalmongering.

At this point, I am genuinely wondering whether anyone remembers why Lomov came to this house.

He wanted to propose marriage. Now he is defending his entire family tree.

And isn't this familiar?

In Indian families, arguments rarely remain limited to the original issue. Someone brings up an old incident, another person mentions a relative, and before you know it, three generations of family history have been dragged into the discussion.

"Tumhare nana ne bhi toh aisa kiya tha!"

"Aur tumhari bua ke baare mein kya bolun?"

Suddenly, everyone has become a historian.

Nobody is listening to anyone, but everyone has something important to say.

4. The Poor Man Gets Insulted and Almost Dies Before Proposing

By now, Lomov is physically distressed. He is already known to be nervous and suffers from various health complaints, and the constant shouting is clearly making things worse.

Natalia and Choobookov continue insulting him until the argument becomes completely unbearable.

NATALIA: Vermin! Liar! Brute!
CHOOBOOKOV: You're a spiteful, double-dealing schemer! So there!

Poor Lomov! He came dressed in a morning coat to propose marriage and is now being called a vermin.

He is clutching his heart, complaining about his leg, and desperately trying to find the door.

CHOOBOOKOV: Ah, my hat! My heart. Where am I? Where's the door? Oooh! I think I'm dying! My foot's totally paralysed.

He finally staggers out.

And Natalia's response?

NATALIA: Go to court! Sue us! Just wait and see!

GIRL, HE WAS GOING TO ASK YOU TO MARRY HIM! 😂

This is what I find so funny about the play. These people are so caught up in winning an argument that they completely forget the actual purpose of the conversation.

The man is practically collapsing, and the property dispute is still more important than the marriage proposal.

5. The Big Revelation: HE CAME TO PROPOSE!

And now comes one of my favourite moments.

After Lomov leaves, Choobookov finally tells Natalia why he had come in the first place.

CHOOBOOKOV: And that clown, that freak had the colossal nerve to ask me for your hand in marriage, and soon. Can you imagine? He wanted to propose.
NATALIA: Propose?
CHOOBOOKOV: Exactly! That's what he came for. To propose to you.
NATALIA: Propose? To me? Why didn't you say so?

WHY DIDN'T YOU SAY SO?

GIRL, HE WAS LITERALLY TRYING TO SAY THAT BEFORE YOU STARTED ARGUING ABOUT THE LAND! 😭😂

And then Natalia completely loses it.

NATALIA: Propose? To me? Ohhh! (collapses into an armchair and wails): Bring him back. Get him. Ohh! Get him!

This is the sort of dramatic revelation that deserves a full Indian television serial treatment.

Imagine the camera zooming into Natalia's face, dramatic music playing in the background, and Choobookov standing there in disbelief.

One moment she is shouting at Lomov, calling him names and threatening to take him to court. The next moment she discovers he wanted to marry her, and suddenly she cannot live without him.

The emotional transition is faster than a television serial's plot twist.

And Choobookov has to go and bring the poor man back.

6. The Second Argument: Now It Is Time to Fight About Dogs

So, Lomov returns.

We have another opportunity for a peaceful conversation. Perhaps this time they will finally discuss the proposal?

Absolutely not.

They somehow start discussing their dogs.

Lomov mentions his dog Guess and complains that the poor animal has gone lame.

LOMOV: Oh, have you heard what bad luck I had? My hound Guess — you know the one — he's gone lame.
NATALIA: What a pity! How did it happen?
LOMOV: I don't know. He must have twisted his leg, or else some other dog bit him. (sighs) My very best hound, not to mention the money! Why, I paid Mironov a hundred and twenty-five roubles for him.
NATALIA: You overpaid him, Ivan Vassilievitch.

And there we go again.

Natalia insists that her dog Leap is better, while Lomov believes Guess is superior.

They start comparing the dogs' qualities, prices, breeds, and abilities. Neither is willing to accept that the other person's dog might be better.

Honestly, I find this hilarious because they have barely managed to settle the marriage proposal, and now they are having another domestic dispute.

Imagine this in an Indian setting.

"Hamare Bruno se achha dog poore mohalla mein nahi milega!"

"Arre, tumhare Bruno ko toh mera Sheru dekh bhi le toh bhaag jayega!"

And then both families start comparing vaccination records, pedigree certificates, training, food habits, and the price they paid.

At this point, I am convinced these two would find something to argue about even if someone handed them a list of things they both liked.

7. Lomov Is Collapsing, and Everyone Is Still Busy Getting Them Married

Now comes the absolute peak of the comedy.

Lomov's health begins deteriorating again. He is so overwhelmed by the argument that he starts feeling extremely unwell.

CHOOBOOKOV: I feel sick! I can't breathe! Air!

Choobookov initially thinks Lomov is dead.

CHOOBOOKOV: He's dead!
NATALIA: What've we done! He's dead. Get a doctor.

And then the panic begins.

CHOOBOOKOV: Who's dead? (glancing at Lomov): He really is dead! Oh, my God! Get some water! Get a doctor!

Choobookov is completely losing his mind. He is holding a glass of water near Lomov's mouth, trying to revive him, and wondering why everything always happens to him.

Then Lomov starts coming around.

LOMOV: Stars... fog... where am I?

And what does Choobookov decide to do?

Get them married immediately!

CHOOBOOKOV: You two'd better hurry up and get married, dammit! She accepts! (joins Lomov's hand with Natalia's) She accepts! My blessings and so forth... Just do me a favour and leave me in peace.

I love how Choobookov has reached the point where he no longer cares about romance, compatibility, or whether these two people even like each other.

He simply wants the marriage completed so that he can finally get some peace.

And honestly, I cannot blame him.

He has witnessed a property dispute, a family scandal session, two separate dog arguments, and a medical emergency.

At this point, even the wedding itself might be less stressful.

8. They Finally Get Engaged! And Immediately Start Fighting Again

So, Natalia accepts the proposal, Choobookov joins their hands, and Lomov is finally informed that he is going to get married.

He is still confused because he has just regained consciousness.

LOMOV: What? (getting up) Who?
CHOOBOOKOV: She accepts. Well? Kiss her and the two of you can go straight to hell.
NATALIA: (moaning): He's alive! I accept, I accept!

And then Lomov kisses Natalia's hands.

LOMOV: What? Who? (kisses Natalia) Enchante! Excuse me, but what's going on? Oh yes, I remember. My heart... stars... I'm very happy. My leg's paralysed.

This poor man has experienced so much in one afternoon that he cannot even properly process his engagement.

And Choobookov finally feels relieved.

But wait. We are not done.

Because Natalia suddenly brings up the dogs again.

NATALIA: But all the same, why don't you finally admit that Guess isn't as good as Leap?
LOMOV: He's much better.
NATALIA: He's worse.
LOMOV: He's better.
NATALIA: Worse! Worse! Worse!

And Choobookov, who has apparently accepted that this is his life now, starts shouting:

CHOOBOOKOV: The launching of marital bliss! Champagne!
CHOOBOOKOV: Champagne! Champagne!

THEY ARE STILL ARGUING ABOUT THE DOGS!

THE ENGAGEMENT HAS BEEN COMPLETED, BUT THE ARGUMENT HAS NOT! 😂😂

This is genuinely one of the funniest endings I have read in a play.

You would expect the story to end with a romantic moment, perhaps a kiss, a promise, or some expression of happiness.

Instead, Chekhov ends with two people continuing the exact behaviour that nearly prevented them from getting married in the first place.

Apparently, this is what marital bliss looks like.

9. The Indian Television Serial Version Would Be Absolute Chaos

I genuinely think this play would be hilarious if adapted into an Indian family drama.

Think about it.

You have two neighbouring families who have known each other for years. There are ancestral properties, old family rivalries, relatives with questionable histories, and a marriage proposal that somehow turns into a full-scale neighbourhood dispute.

Now imagine replacing the Oxen Meadows with a piece of ancestral land.

Instead of calmly discussing who owns it, both families start producing old property papers, handwritten agreements, land records, and documents that nobody has seen in thirty years.

Someone's uncle suddenly remembers that the land was promised to his grandfather. Another relative claims that the paperwork was manipulated. Someone else starts talking about corruption in the local land registry.

And just when everyone thinks the matter is settled, an elderly aunt appears with a completely different version of events.

Then comes the family gossip.

"Tumhare dadaji ne bhi toh hamare parivaar ke saath dhokha kiya tha!"

"Aur tumhare chacha ka woh purana kissa bhool gaye kya?"

Suddenly, everyone is bringing up scandals from decades ago.

And imagine the dramatic music every time someone mentions a new family secret.

Then, after all this, the girl discovers that the man actually came to propose to her.

Dramatic zoom. Shocked expression. Background music.

"Woh... mujhse shaadi karna chahta tha?"

And then she starts crying and asks everyone to bring him back.

Finally, the two families decide to arrange the wedding, but before the engagement ceremony can even begin, the couple starts arguing about whose dog is better.

And of course, the relatives join in.

One person supports Bruno. Another supports Sheru. Someone brings up how much each dog cost. Someone else starts comparing their breeds.

Meanwhile, the father is standing in the middle, shouting, "Bas karo! Shaadi hone do pehle!"

Honestly, the only thing missing from this play is a dramatic family member entering the room and saying, "Yeh shaadi nahi ho sakti!"

Chekhov would probably have had enough material for another three acts.

10. What Is Chekhov Actually Making Fun Of?

Beneath all the ridiculous arguments, there is actually a rather interesting observation about human nature.

The play makes fun of how easily people allow their pride to take control of ordinary situations.

The Oxen Meadows are not merely a piece of land. They become a matter of personal honour. Neither Lomov nor Natalia wants to accept that the other person might be right.

The same thing happens with their dogs. The actual quality of the animals almost becomes irrelevant. What matters is proving that one's own dog is superior.

Their arguments are not really about finding the truth. They are about winning.

And this is what makes the play so amusing and relatable. People often spend enormous amounts of energy defending things that are not particularly important simply because they do not want to lose an argument.

Chekhov also makes fun of the way marriage can be treated as a social arrangement rather than an emotional decision. Lomov is primarily concerned with finding a suitable wife, and the families seem perfectly comfortable with the idea of arranging the marriage because their social and financial circumstances are compatible.

There is very little romantic conversation between the two prospective partners. Instead, we get property disputes, family gossip, and arguments about dogs.

The comedy comes from the contrast between what should be happening and what actually happens.

A marriage proposal should ideally be a happy occasion. Here, it becomes an opportunity for everyone to display their stubbornness.

11. The Characters Are Almost Perfectly Matched in Their Stubbornness

Something I particularly enjoy is that none of the three characters is particularly reasonable.

Lomov is nervous, physically weak, and constantly worried about his health. But he is also incredibly stubborn. He can barely manage his own emotions, yet he is perfectly capable of spending an entire afternoon arguing about land and dogs.

Natalia is equally determined to prove that she is right. She is quick to interrupt Lomov, fiercely defends her family's property, and does not hesitate to insult him when the argument becomes heated.

Choobookov is perhaps the funniest of the three. Initially, he is delighted by the possibility of the marriage. But the moment the argument begins, he joins in with enthusiasm.

He insults Lomov's family, participates in the shouting, and eventually becomes desperate to get the two of them married.

It is almost as though he has realised that these two people deserve each other.

And honestly, he might be right.

They are both stubborn, argumentative, competitive, and incapable of letting the smallest disagreement go.

Perfect couple material! 😂

12. Final Thoughts: A Marriage Proposal Where Nobody Actually Proposes Properly

I think what makes A Marriage Proposal such an entertaining play is its simplicity.

There is no complicated plot, no extraordinary setting, and no major mystery to solve. We simply have three people who cannot seem to agree on anything.

Yet Chekhov manages to turn these ordinary disagreements into an incredibly funny commentary on human behaviour.

The play explores pride, stubbornness, social expectations, family relationships, marriage, and the ridiculous importance people sometimes attach to winning arguments.

What I particularly like is that the characters are not presented as extraordinary villains. They are ordinary people with ordinary insecurities and exaggerated reactions.

We all know people who behave a little like Lomov, Natalia, or Choobookov. People who can turn a small disagreement into a full family discussion, bring up things that happened twenty years ago, and somehow find a reason to argue even when everything is going perfectly well.

And perhaps that is why the play feels so familiar despite being written in nineteenth-century Russia.

The setting may be different, the names may be Russian, and the arguments may involve Oxen Meadows and hunting dogs, but the human behaviour is remarkably recognisable.

Replace the ancestral land with a disputed piece of property, add a few relatives, throw in some old family gossip, and give everyone a microphone.

You have an Indian family drama ready to go.

And the ending? Absolutely perfect.

Two people finally agree to get married, their father celebrates, and they immediately return to arguing about whose dog is better.

Honestly, somebody please make this into a Hindi television serial. I would watch it. 😂💜
