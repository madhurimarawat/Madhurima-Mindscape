Importance Is Temporary and Subjective

November 7, 2026

Something or someone can be extremely important to you at a particular point in life, but as time passes, circumstances change, and priorities shift, that same thing may no longer hold the importance it once did. This applies to friendships, education, photographs, projects, travel experiences, and even the little things we become attached to.

I have been thinking about this idea from a personal perspective. We often become so attached to things because of the importance they hold at a particular moment that we forget one simple fact: their importance may not remain the same forever.

Rather than holding on to everything, perhaps we should learn to appreciate things while they matter, let go when their purpose is fulfilled, and make the most of the experiences we are living through right now.

"Importance is temporary and subjective. Enjoy what matters today, because tomorrow may bring a different meaning."

1. Importance in Relationships

You can be the most important person in someone's life at a particular point in time. You may be their closest friend, the person they share everything with, or the one they turn to whenever they need help. But after some time, things can change. People get busy with their lives, develop new priorities, meet new people, and move in different directions.

Think about school friendships. During those years, your school friends may have been an essential part of your everyday life. You had homework to complete together, played around, talked about random things, and shared countless little moments. They were important because you were all part of the same routine and environment.

Then school ended. Everyone moved on to different colleges, cities, courses, and responsibilities. The same thing can happen with college friendships. People who once spent hours together may eventually become occupied with their own lives.

This change can be painful, especially when you still consider someone important but no longer receive the same attention from them. However, there is something we need to understand: relationships require effort from both sides.

If you are important to someone, they will make an effort to spend time with you. Everyone has responsibilities, but people generally find time for the relationships they genuinely want to maintain. If someone repeatedly makes no effort to stay connected, you should not have to keep begging for their attention.

Never beg someone to stay. Never force someone to make you a priority. Instead, focus on yourself and the life you are building. If you feel lonely, try making new friends, exploring new hobbies, starting projects, or discovering activities that make you happy. And if you do not find the right company immediately, learn to enjoy your own solitude.

There is nothing wrong with appreciating old friendships while accepting that people and circumstances change. Some people are meant to be part of a particular chapter of your life, and that does not make the chapter any less meaningful.

"Never beg someone to stay in your life. Appreciate the time you shared, but do not forget to live your own life."

2. Importance in Education

Education is another example of how importance changes with time. Think about the subjects, lessons, books, and notes you study in a particular class. During that academic year, they may be extremely important. You need them for assignments, examinations, projects, and understanding new concepts.

But once that stage is over, those same books may no longer be useful to you in the way they once were. You have moved to another class, completed a degree, or started learning something entirely different.

Instead of keeping every old book and notebook indefinitely, consider giving them to someone who can use them. If you have a junior, a younger student, or someone who needs those resources, pass them along.

A book that is no longer useful to you might be extremely valuable to someone else. Your old notes could help another student prepare for an examination, understand a difficult topic, or save money on study materials.

Of course, you can keep books that hold sentimental value or that you genuinely plan to revisit. The idea is not to get rid of everything. It is to recognise when something has served its purpose and allow it to be useful somewhere else.

"A book that has completed its journey with you can become the beginning of someone else's learning."

3. Organise Your Digital Space

We often talk about organising physical spaces, but digital organisation is equally important. Our phones and computers accumulate photographs, screenshots, documents, downloads, and countless files over time.

Consider photographs, for example. Sometimes you get dressed, look particularly good, and feel like taking several pictures. You may end up taking fifty or even a hundred photographs because you like how you look that day. This is completely normal.

However, once you have taken those photographs, do you really need to keep every single one of them?

Select one or two of your favourite pictures, perhaps a few more if they genuinely capture different moments, and delete the unnecessary duplicates. You do not need ten nearly identical photographs just because you liked how you looked in that particular moment.

The same applies to screenshots, downloaded images, temporary documents, and other digital clutter. Keep what is useful or meaningful, organise it properly, and remove what you no longer need.

Sometimes we become so attached to keeping everything that we forget the actual purpose of storing these things. Having a thousand photographs does not necessarily mean having a thousand meaningful memories.

"Organise your digital memories. Keep what matters, not every duplicate of what once mattered."

4. Keep New Things Accessible

Another useful habit is organising documents using a new-to-old approach. Keep your latest and most frequently needed files accessible, while moving older documents into appropriate folders or archives.

For example, if you are currently studying or working on a particular project, the latest notes, assignments, and documents should be easy to find. Older materials can be stored separately instead of occupying the most visible space.

This approach works for educational documents, certificates, project files, photographs, and personal records. You can organise them by year, category, subject, or purpose.

There is no need to delete every old document. Some files may be important for future reference. The idea is simply to distinguish between what you currently need and what you might need later.

When everything is mixed together, even finding a simple file can become frustrating. A little organisation can save you a lot of time.

"Keep what you need today within reach, and give yesterday's things a proper place."

5. Enjoy the Present Moment

If importance is temporary and subjective, then perhaps the most important thing we can do is enjoy something while it still matters to us.

A person, a book, a degree, a travel experience, a beautiful view, a joke, a project, a website, a cup of coffee, or a delicious dish can all hold a special place in our lives. But their importance may change as we move through different stages.

You may spend years working towards a degree. During that period, examinations, assignments, and academic achievements may occupy most of your attention. Once you graduate, you move towards a different phase of life. The degree remains an achievement, but your everyday priorities change.

The same applies to experiences. You may be visiting a beautiful place today, enjoying a conversation with someone, or working on a project that makes you incredibly happy. These moments are happening right now. You may remember them later, but you cannot return to the exact same moment.

So why not enjoy them while they are happening?

Instead of constantly thinking about what comes next, take some time to appreciate what is already around you. Enjoy the food you are eating, the people you are spending time with, the place you are visiting, and the little things that make your day better.

"Do not wait for a moment to become a memory before you realise how beautiful it was."

6. Experience the Place, Not Just the Photos

This is something I particularly want to talk about when it comes to travelling. Whenever you visit a new place, try not to spend the entire time taking photographs.

You can decide on a reasonable limit for the number of photographs you want to take. Personally, I would recommend starting with a maximum of around twenty-four pictures for a trip, depending on its duration and the kind of experience you are having.

Take a few pictures of yourself, capture the landscape, photograph the people you are travelling with, and preserve a few details that make the place special. Once you have done that, put your phone away for some time and actually enjoy where you are.

Think about what often happens when we visit a mountain, a hill station, an ocean, or a beach. We reach the destination and immediately start taking pictures. We take photographs from different angles, check how we look, adjust the camera, and try to capture everything perfectly.

Sometimes it almost feels as though we have travelled to a place just to collect photographs, upload them as social media updates, and show other people where we have been.

There is absolutely nothing wrong with taking photographs or sharing them online. It is a personal choice, and photographs can be wonderful reminders of our experiences. However, the problem begins when capturing the place becomes more important than experiencing it.

Imagine standing in front of a huge ocean. You can hear the waves, feel the breeze, and see the water stretching far into the distance. Why not spend some time simply looking at it?

If you are visiting mountains, admire their vastness. Look at the deep valleys, the changing colours of the sky, the surrounding landscape, and the little details that make the place beautiful. Notice the people around you. Enjoy the company of your family or friends. Even if you are travelling alone, give yourself time to appreciate the experience.

These are the memories that stay with you. A photograph can remind you of a place, but the actual experience includes so much more: the atmosphere, the sounds, the people, the emotions, and the feeling of being there.

"Do not travel only to capture a place. Travel to experience it."

7. Take Pictures, Then Move On

I love photography. I enjoy taking pictures of flowers, landscapes, and beautiful little things that catch my attention. But over time, I have started changing the way I approach photography.

For example, when I see a beautiful rose or another flower, I may take one or two pictures. Once I have captured the image I like, I put my phone away and spend some time looking at the flower itself.

I admire its colours, its petals, and the little details that make it beautiful. I do not need to keep taking photographs from every possible angle. I have already captured a picture that can remind me of the moment. Now I can simply enjoy what is in front of me.

The same applies to travelling. I may take a few photographs of myself, the landscape, or something interesting around me. But after that, I want to look around and enjoy the place instead of constantly checking my phone.

Sometimes I even feel that the desire to take another photograph is unnecessary. I might already have a perfectly good picture, but I still feel the urge to take more because I want everything to look perfect.

That is where I think we need to pause. Nature is too beautiful to be reduced to a collection of photographs. It is meant to be admired, experienced, and enjoyed. A mountain does not become more beautiful because you have taken fifty photographs of it. The ocean does not become more meaningful because you have captured every wave.

You can take a photograph, appreciate it, and move on. Give yourself permission to enjoy the actual experience instead of trying to capture every possible detail.

"Take a picture to remember the moment, but do not forget to experience the moment itself."

8. A Photo Is Not the Whole Experience

There is a difference between capturing a moment and having an entire experience. A photograph can preserve a particular view, expression, or scene, but it cannot contain everything that happened around it.

Think about a beautiful sunset. You can take a picture of the sky, but the photograph cannot fully preserve the feeling of standing there, watching the colours change, feeling the breeze, listening to the surroundings, and enjoying the peacefulness of that moment.

Similarly, a photograph with your friends may remind you of a gathering, but it cannot capture every conversation, every joke, every laugh, or the little things that made that day special.

Sometimes we become so focused on getting the perfect photograph that we forget to enjoy the actual experience. We keep adjusting the camera, checking the results, retaking pictures, and thinking about how the final image will look.

In doing so, we may end up giving away part of the experience just to capture a moment. We focus so much on the photograph that we pay less attention to the place, the people, and the feelings that make the experience meaningful.

This is one of the reasons I have gradually started using my phone less when I visit new places. I still enjoy photography, but I want the experience to remain more important than the number of pictures I bring home.

I am also a fairly private person when it comes to social media. I do not share a large number of photographs online. I prefer keeping many of my pictures on my phone, where they can simply remain personal memories.

And honestly, that is perfectly fine. Not every photograph needs to be uploaded, and not every experience needs to be displayed for other people.

"A photograph captures a moment. A memory carries the experience."

9. Let Go of Photo Obsession

There was a time when I was extremely particular about keeping all my photographs. I wanted every picture to remain saved. Even if one photograph got deleted accidentally, I would become incredibly upset.

I would think about that missing picture and wonder how it could have been deleted. It felt as though losing one photograph meant losing something very important.

But eventually, I started thinking about the temporary and subjective nature of importance. That photograph was extremely important to me at that particular time. But would it really hold the same importance after a few months or years?

Perhaps I would look at it later, feel happy, remember the day, and then move on. That is not a bad thing. Photographs are wonderful reminders, but they do not need to become something we are afraid of losing.

I realised that I do not need to keep every photograph to preserve every memory. I can keep a few meaningful pictures, delete unnecessary duplicates, and still remember the experience.

This does not mean that photographs are useless or that we should stop taking them. I still enjoy photography, and I still like having pictures that remind me of special moments. The point is to avoid becoming so obsessed with preserving every image that we forget to enjoy our lives.

Keep some photographs that remind you of beautiful moments. Look back at them, smile, and appreciate those memories. But do not feel that every single image needs to stay forever.

"Keep photographs as reminders of your memories, not as replacements for the experiences themselves."

10. Keep Memories, Not Just Photos

This is perhaps the most important thing I want to convey through this article: keep memories, not just photographs.

A photograph can remind you of a particular place or person, but the memory is much bigger than the image. It includes what you felt, what you experienced, what you learned, and the little details that made that moment special.

You may visit a beautiful destination and take only a handful of pictures. Years later, you might look at one of them and remember the entire experience: the journey, the people you travelled with, the conversations, the weather, and the beautiful scenery.

That is what makes a photograph meaningful. It reminds you of something you actually experienced. The picture is a small part of the memory, not the entire memory itself.

So take pictures, preserve the ones you love, and enjoy looking back at them. But do not let the process of taking photographs take away the joy of being present.

Sometimes the best thing you can do is simply put your phone away, look around, and appreciate where you are. Let the beauty of nature, the company of people, and the experience itself become part of your memories.

"Do not just collect photographs of your life. Make sure you are actually living the moments you want to remember."

💜 Final Thoughts

The idea that importance is temporary and subjective is something I have been thinking about from different perspectives. It applies to people, education, photographs, documents, experiences, and almost everything we become attached to during different stages of life.

Something can be incredibly important today and become less significant tomorrow. That does not mean it was meaningless. It simply means that life moves forward, circumstances change, and our priorities evolve.

Appreciate people while they are part of your life, make good use of the things you have, pass along what someone else can benefit from, and do not become unnecessarily attached to things that have already served their purpose.

Most importantly, enjoy the present. Whether you are travelling, spending time with someone, working on a project, enjoying a beautiful view, or simply having a peaceful day, give yourself the opportunity to experience it fully.

I also want to connect this thought with one of my earlier articles, Unfinished Projects. That article explores the same idea from a different perspective. Some projects are extremely important to us at a particular point in time. We invest our love, effort, creativity, and countless hours into them. But eventually, we move on and abandon them.

Sometimes these projects remain with us like old, abandoned, haunted buildings. We cannot easily delete them because they hold memories of the time and effort we invested in creating them. Their importance may have changed, but the memories attached to them remain.

While Unfinished Projects discusses this idea through projects and the effort we put into them, this article looks at it from a more personal perspective. Both are reminders that importance changes, and learning to accept that change is part of growing.

"Enjoy what matters today. Appreciate what mattered yesterday. And make space for what tomorrow may bring."
