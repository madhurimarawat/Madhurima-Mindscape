The Canterbury Tales By Geoffrey Chaucer

November 10, 2026

I have to say, "The Canterbury Tales" is one of those works that feels surprisingly entertaining once you get into it. Honestly, imagine an ancient gossip group where a bunch of people are travelling together and everyone has a story to tell. Someone is talking about this, someone is commenting on that, and before you know it, you have a whole collection of personalities, opinions, experiences, and dramatic stories. That is more or less the atmosphere of "The Canterbury Tales".

The poem is built around a pilgrimage to Canterbury. A group of travellers are going on a religious journey, and they decide to entertain one another by telling stories along the way. The General Prologue introduces these travellers, giving us a glimpse of their appearance, behaviour, professions, personalities, and social positions. And this is where Chaucer becomes particularly interesting.

Canterbury Tales Illustration(Source)

The General Prologue is basically a very sophisticated roast! The funny thing is that you may not recognise it immediately. Chaucer describes some characters so beautifully that, at first, you might think he is genuinely praising them. Then you read the description again and realise that he is actually pointing out their hypocrisy, vanity, or questionable behaviour. It is a wonderful use of irony.

However, I would not call Chaucer's irony particularly cruel. It is often quite gentle and humorous. He shows us the contrast between what a person is supposed to be and what that person actually is. A religious figure may be expected to live a spiritual life, for example, but their behaviour may reveal a completely different set of priorities.

Another thing I absolutely love is that every character is an individual and, at the same time, represents a particular social type or class. The Prioress is a character in her own right, but she also helps us understand the problems and corruption within the medieval Church. The Monk is another individual, but his behaviour also reflects the contrast between religious expectations and worldly desires.

And then there is the Wife of Bath! She is easily one of the most entertaining characters. She is confident, outspoken, experienced, and completely comfortable with expressing her opinions. She is often discussed as a proto-feminist figure because of her strong female voice and her insistence on the value of personal experience.

What makes the whole work even more enjoyable is its storytelling structure. Not every tale is completed, and some stories are left unfinished, but the collection still feels like a lively gathering where everyone has something to contribute. The stories are often connected to the characters' own personalities and experiences. It is almost like a family gathering, except that these people are fellow travellers rather than family members!

Of course, the language can be difficult at first. Chaucer wrote in Middle English, which is quite different from the English we use today. Words such as "whan", "shoures", and "soote" may look unfamiliar, and the sentence structure can take some getting used to. But once you read the original lines alongside a modern interpretation, the language becomes much easier to understand.

For me, "The Canterbury Tales" is not simply a collection of medieval stories. It is a lively picture of society, human behaviour, hypocrisy, ambition, relationships, and everyday experiences. Let's look at some of its most interesting passages and characters.

About the Poet

Geoffrey Chaucer was an English poet and author who lived during the fourteenth century. He is often called the "Father of English Poetry" because of his important contribution to the development of English literature. His writing provides a remarkable picture of medieval life, society, culture, and human behaviour.

"The Canterbury Tales" is his most celebrated work. It is written primarily in Middle English and presents a group of pilgrims travelling from the Tabard Inn in Southwark to the shrine of Saint Thomas Becket at Canterbury. During their journey, they tell stories to entertain one another.

One of Chaucer's greatest strengths is his characterisation. He introduces people from different professions and social backgrounds, including religious figures, members of the aristocracy, merchants, craftsmen, and ordinary working people. Through their descriptions and stories, he creates a broad picture of medieval English society.

Chaucer is particularly admired for his humour, irony, realism, and understanding of human nature. Rather than simply describing society as it should be, he shows people as they actually behave, with all their contradictions, weaknesses, ambitions, and peculiarities.

The Opening: Spring, Pilgrimage, and the Storytelling Group

Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote
The droghte of March hath perced to the roote
And bathed every veyne in swich licour
Of which vertu engendred is the flour,

Chaucer begins the General Prologue with a beautiful description of spring. April brings gentle showers, the dryness of March disappears, flowers begin to grow, and nature comes alive again. The atmosphere is full of freshness, renewal, and movement.

The opening is important because spring is also the season when people feel encouraged to travel on pilgrimages. Chaucer uses this natural change to introduce the journey to Canterbury. So, the description of spring is not simply decorative. It gives us the reason behind the movement of the travellers and establishes the pilgrimage frame of the entire work.

And honestly, this is where the ancient gossip-group idea becomes so much fun! Imagine a group of people travelling together. They have different professions, different personalities, different opinions, and different experiences. They are not necessarily friends or relatives, but they are sharing a journey, and eventually, they begin sharing stories too.

It feels surprisingly familiar. Think about a family gathering or a long journey with a group of people. Someone starts telling a story, another person has a different experience to share, and soon everyone is involved. That is the kind of lively atmosphere Chaucer creates.

Some words:

Shoures soote: Sweet or gentle showers of rain.

Droghte: Drought or dryness.

Pilgrimage: A religious journey to a sacred place.

A journey becomes much more interesting when everyone travelling with you has a story to tell.

The Prioress and the Monk: Chaucer's Gentle Roast

One of the most entertaining things about the General Prologue is Chaucer's use of irony. He introduces characters in a way that may initially sound like admiration, but when we look more carefully, we begin to notice the criticism hidden beneath the description.

Take the Prioress, for example. She is a nun and is therefore expected to represent religious devotion, discipline, and spiritual seriousness. Chaucer describes her as a gentle and delicate woman. She is so sensitive that she cries when she sees a mouse caught in a trap or hurt.

At first, you might think, "Oh, she is such a kind and compassionate person!" But then you notice the other details. She is very conscious of her manners, appearance, table etiquette, and courtly behaviour. She speaks French, particularly the French associated with the Stratford-at-Bow school, rather than the prestigious French of Paris.

This is where the irony becomes interesting. A Prioress is expected to devote herself to religious life, but Chaucer presents someone who seems rather interested in aristocratic manners and worldly refinement. The description is not simply a compliment. It gently exposes the contrast between her religious position and her actual priorities.

The Monk offers another example. A monk is traditionally expected to follow a disciplined religious life, but Chaucer's Monk enjoys hunting, fine clothing, and worldly pleasures. He does not seem particularly interested in strict monastic discipline.

What I love is that Chaucer does not need to make a direct accusation. He simply describes their behaviour and allows us to recognise the contradiction. The humour is subtle, almost like a polite roast. You may need to read the description twice to realise that the apparent praise is actually criticism!

Some words:

Prioress: A nun who is the head of a religious house or convent.

Ecclesiastical: Related to the Church or its religious organisation.

Irony: A literary technique in which the intended meaning differs from, or contrasts with, the literal expression.

Chaucer's cleverest criticism often sounds like a compliment until you read it again.

The Wife of Bath: A Confident and Independent Woman

Experience, though noon auctoritee
Were in this world, is right ynogh for me.

Now, if you ask me to choose one of the most entertaining characters in "The Canterbury Tales", the Wife of Bath would definitely be among my first choices. She is outspoken, confident, experienced, and not afraid to express what she thinks.

She has been married five times and speaks openly about marriage, relationships, power, and the experiences she has gained through her husbands. She is not presented as a shy or passive woman who simply accepts what society expects of her. She knows what she wants and is quite comfortable discussing it.

The quotation above is particularly important because she argues that personal experience has value even when it is not supported by written authority. In other words, she does not believe that books and traditional opinions are the only sources of knowledge. Her own life has taught her things, and she is confident enough to defend that experience.

This is one reason why the Wife of Bath is often described as a proto-feminist character. She gives a strong female voice to a medieval literary work. She speaks about women's desires, independence, marriage, and authority in a way that feels remarkably bold for the period.

There is also something quite amusing about her personality. She has married several times, has experience of managing relationships, and is very aware of the financial and social advantages that marriage can bring. She is clever, practical, and sometimes manipulative. You could even call her a kind of medieval femme fatale!

What makes her especially interesting is that she is not simply a symbol of women or feminism. She is also an individual with her own personality, opinions, contradictions, and sense of humour. Chaucer gives her a voice that is difficult to ignore.

Some words:

Auctoritee: Authority, particularly the authority of books, traditional learning, or established opinion.

Experience: Personal knowledge gained through living and doing something.

Proto-feminist: A person or character who expresses ideas associated with feminism before the modern feminist movement developed.

Femme fatale: A confident and attractive woman who is often portrayed as powerful, persuasive, or dangerously influential.

She does not ask permission to have an opinion. She simply has one, and she makes sure everyone hears it.

The Pardoner: Hypocrisy and Corruption of the Church

Radix malorum est cupiditas.

Another particularly interesting character is the Pardoner. He represents one of Chaucer's strongest examples of religious hypocrisy and corruption. A Pardoner was associated with the practice of granting pardons and distributing religious indulgences. However, Chaucer's character is far more interested in money than in genuine spiritual service.

The most amusing part is that the Pardoner openly admits his own greed. He preaches against the very weakness that controls his life. His famous statement, "Radix malorum est cupiditas", means "The root of all evil is greed." Yet he uses religious preaching to make money from other people's fears and beliefs.

This is a wonderful example of irony. A religious figure is supposed to guide people towards moral goodness, but the Pardoner uses religion as a way of exploiting them. His words may sound spiritually meaningful, but his intentions are completely selfish.

What I find particularly interesting is that Chaucer does not restrict his criticism to one individual. Through characters like the Pardoner and the Monk, he draws attention to the wider problem of ecclesiastical corruption. He shows the gap between the ideals of the Church and the behaviour of some of the people working within it.

And this is exactly what makes the General Prologue so clever. Each character is a person, but each one also represents a particular profession, social group, or human weakness. Chaucer creates individuals who make us think about society as a whole.

Some words:

Radix malorum est cupiditas: The root of all evil is greed.

Pardoner: A religious figure associated with granting pardons and distributing indulgences.

Indulgence: In medieval Catholic practice, a remission of temporal punishment for sins under prescribed religious conditions.

Hypocrisy: Pretending to follow moral or religious principles while behaving in a way that contradicts them.

The most effective religious preaching means very little when the preacher does not practise what he teaches.

Some More Interesting Aspects of The Canterbury Tales

One of the most beautiful things about "The Canterbury Tales" is that Chaucer's characters work on more than one level. The Knight, the Prioress, the Monk, the Wife of Bath, and the Pardoner are all individual personalities, but they also represent different professions and sections of medieval society.

Their clothes, manners, speech, professions, and behaviour reveal a great deal about them. Chaucer uses these details to create a realistic picture of medieval society. He does not simply tell us what a character is like. He gives us enough details to form our own opinions.

The Knight, for example, represents the ideal of medieval chivalry. He is described as a worthy and honourable man, while other characters reveal the gap between social expectations and actual behaviour. This contrast is one of the foundations of Chaucer's satire.

He was a verray, parfit gentil knight.

This is one of the most important descriptions in the General Prologue. The Knight represents the ideal qualities of his social class: honour, truth, generosity, courtesy, and nobility. His character also gives us a standard against which we can understand the more questionable behaviour of others.

The storytelling structure is equally important. The pilgrims do not all tell stories that are identical in subject or style. Their tales often reflect their personalities, social positions, interests, and life experiences. Some stories are completed, while others remain unfinished, but together they create a lively collection of different voices.

Some important literary devices:

Irony: Chaucer often appears to praise a character while subtly revealing their weaknesses and contradictions.

Satire: Humour, exaggeration, and criticism are used to expose social weaknesses, hypocrisy, and corruption.

Realism: Characters are presented with recognisable human habits, ambitions, flaws, and experiences.

Characterisation: Chaucer develops personalities through appearance, clothing, speech, behaviour, and profession.

Frame Narrative: A larger story provides the structure within which several smaller stories are told. The pilgrimage provides this framework for the tales.

Social Satire: Chaucer uses individual characters to comment on wider social groups and institutions, particularly the Church and medieval social hierarchy.

Dress reveals class, behaviour reveals character, irony reveals Chaucer's satire.

My Favourite Quotations and Their Meanings

If there is one thing I would recommend while reading "The Canterbury Tales", it is saving some of its most important quotations. They are useful for understanding the characters, themes, and literary techniques of the poem.

Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote
The droghte of March hath perced to the roote ...

This is the famous opening of the General Prologue. It introduces spring, renewal, and the beginning of the pilgrimage. It is particularly useful when discussing the structure and setting of the poem.

He was a verray, parfit gentil knight.

This line presents the Knight as an ideal representative of medieval chivalry. He embodies honour, truth, nobility, and courtesy.

Experience, though noon auctoritee
Were in this world, is right ynogh for me.

The Wife of Bath argues that personal experience is a valuable source of knowledge, even when it is not supported by traditional written authority. It reveals her confidence and independent personality.

Radix malorum est cupiditas.

This means "The root of all evil is greed." The Pardoner's use of this statement is deeply ironic because he preaches against greed while being greedy himself.

Another memorable observation from Chaucer's writing is his understanding of human emotion. He recognises that people do not always express their suffering openly:

For certes, many a man so hard is of his herte,
He may not wepe, though him sore smerte.

These lines suggest that some people are so emotionally restrained that they cannot cry even when they are deeply hurt. They show Chaucer's interest in the complexity of human feelings.

I particularly like how these quotations introduce different aspects of the work: the pilgrimage, medieval ideals, women's voices, religious hypocrisy, and human psychology.

Chaucer's characters are memorable because they remind us of people we still recognise in everyday life.

In my view, "The Canterbury Tales" is one of those works that becomes much more enjoyable when we stop thinking of it only as an ancient literary text and start imagining the people behind the stories. It is almost like joining a group of travellers who have gathered together and decided to entertain one another with stories from their lives.

What I particularly love is Chaucer's way of presenting people. Every character has an individual personality, but at the same time, each one represents a larger social group or type. The Prioress and the Monk reveal the contrast between religious expectations and worldly behaviour, while the Pardoner exposes the hypocrisy and greed that can exist within religious institutions.

And the Wife of Bath is simply wonderful to read! Her confidence, independence, and insistence on personal experience make her one of the most striking female characters in medieval literature. She may be complicated and sometimes manipulative, but she is certainly not someone who disappears quietly into the background.

I also think Chaucer's irony deserves special attention. His criticism is often so subtle that you may initially mistake it for admiration. You read about a character, think that Chaucer is praising them, and then suddenly realise that he has been pointing out their weaknesses all along. That is what makes the General Prologue such an enjoyable example of social satire.

Of course, the language can be challenging. Middle English is very different from modern English, and the unfamiliar vocabulary and sentence structures may make the first reading difficult. But I would genuinely recommend reading the original text alongside a modern translation or interpretation. Read it once, understand the meaning, and then return to the original lines. The second or third reading often makes a surprising difference.

Not every tale is finished, and not every character gets the same amount of attention, but that does not take away from the pleasure of reading the work. The collection feels lively, varied, and wonderfully human. It is full of people who have different experiences and different ways of looking at the world.

For me, that is the real charm of "The Canterbury Tales". It is not just a collection of medieval stories. It is a gathering of voices, personalities, ambitions, flaws, and experiences. And honestly, the idea that a group of travellers can turn a pilgrimage into something resembling a very entertaining storytelling session is quite brilliant!
