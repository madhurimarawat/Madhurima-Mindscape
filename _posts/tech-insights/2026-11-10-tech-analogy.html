Technical Concepts Using Analogy

November 10, 2026

Have you ever tried explaining a technical concept to someone who has never opened VS Code, written a line of code, or even heard of a Git repository?

It can be surprisingly difficult. Sometimes, the concept itself is not complicated, but the terminology makes it sound that way.

Technology Icons Illustration (Source)

That's where analogies come in. Instead of explaining something using technical definitions, you connect it to something people already understand from everyday life.

For example, explaining GitHub through a makeup organiser, neural networks through a family preparing dinner, or cloud computing through public transport can make an unfamiliar concept much easier to understand.

"The best way to explain something complicated is to connect it to something familiar."

Here are a few everyday analogies I came up with to explain some common technical concepts.

💄 1. GitHub and Git Branches: A Makeup Organiser

Imagine you have a makeup organiser. You don't throw all your makeup, jewellery, watches, and accessories into one big compartment, do you?

You probably have:

  • A separate pouch for lipsticks.
  • A separate section for earrings.
  • A dedicated box for watches.
  • Another compartment for bracelets.

Everything has its own place, making it easier to find things and preventing one item from getting mixed up with another.

Git branches work in a similar way. You can think of each branch as a separate compartment where you work on a particular feature or change without immediately affecting the main version of your project.

For example, you might have:

  • Main branch: The finished, stable version of your project.
  • Feature branch: A separate space to build a new feature.
  • Bug-fix branch: A separate space to fix an issue.

Once the work is ready, you can merge the changes into the main branch. It's a little like organising your accessories first and then deciding which ones belong together.

Now think about having separate boxes for different types of jewellery. One box might contain heavy traditional jewellery, while another contains simple everyday accessories. You can even add labels and descriptions so you remember what's inside.

That's similar to having separate Git repositories for different projects. Each repository has its own code, purpose, and history. A clear repository name and README file act like labels on those boxes, helping everyone understand what's inside.

The takeaway: Git helps you organise and track changes, while branches and repositories help keep different pieces of work organised.

🥕 2. Artificial Intelligence: A Vegetable Seller's Calculations

Imagine a vegetable seller at the end of the day.

They have a basket full of vegetables. Some are fresh, some are slightly damaged, and some might become spoiled if they aren't sold soon.

The seller needs to make several calculations:

  • How much did each vegetable cost?
  • What price should it be sold for?
  • Should the price be reduced for vegetables that are getting old?
  • How much can be earned without making a loss?

Instead of calculating everything manually, imagine having a very efficient assistant who can quickly compare all these details and suggest a suitable selling price.

That's one way to imagine the computational side of AI. Computers can process large amounts of information, identify patterns, and calculate possible outcomes much faster than we can manually.

Of course, AI is much more than a calculator. It can also learn patterns from examples and use those patterns to generate predictions, recommendations, or responses.

The takeaway: AI can help process information and make predictions based on patterns, but the quality of its output depends on the data, model, and task.

📚 3. How AI Learns: Learning From Examples

Let's say you want to teach a child to identify apples.

You show them several pictures of apples: red apples, green apples, small apples, and large apples. After seeing enough examples, the child starts recognising what an apple looks like.

AI learning can work in a somewhat similar way.

A machine learning model is trained using examples. It identifies patterns in the data and uses what it has learned to make predictions when it encounters new information.

For instance, if a model is shown thousands of pictures labelled as cats and dogs, it can gradually learn patterns that help it distinguish between the two.

It isn't memorising a simple definition of a cat. It is learning patterns from the examples it has been given.

The takeaway: Machine learning allows computers to learn patterns from data rather than relying only on manually written rules.

🍲 4. Neural Networks: A Family Preparing Dinner Together

Imagine a family preparing dinner together.

One person chops the vegetables. Another washes them. Someone peels the potatoes, another prepares the spices, and someone else takes care of cooking.

Each person handles a small part of the process. Eventually, all those individual contributions come together to create one complete meal.

A neural network has a somewhat similar structure.

It consists of interconnected units called neurons, arranged in layers. Each neuron performs a small mathematical calculation and passes its result to other neurons in the network.

As information moves through the layers, the network combines these calculations to produce an output.

But what happens if the result isn't correct?

Let's say someone accidentally peels away almost the entire potato. The next time, you give them feedback: "Hey, we needed the potato, not just the skin!" They take that feedback into account and try to do better next time.

Neural networks have a training process that is somewhat similar. During training, the model compares its prediction with the expected answer and calculates how far off it was. This difference is called the error or loss.

Using a process called backpropagation, the network works backwards to calculate how its internal parameters contributed to the error. An optimisation algorithm then adjusts those parameters to improve future predictions.

It's not literally learning from feedback like a person, but the dinner-preparation analogy gives us a simple way to understand how smaller operations work together and how training improves the overall result.

The takeaway: A neural network combines calculations across connected layers, and training adjusts its parameters to reduce errors.

🛒 5. Database: A DMart Store

Think about a large supermarket like DMart.

It has records of its products, prices, stock levels, suppliers, and sales. Imagine if the store had no proper record of anything. How would the staff know which products are available, which ones are running low, or what needs to be restocked?

That would be a complete mess!

A database works in a similar way. It stores and organises information so that applications can retrieve, update, and manage it when needed.

For example, an online shopping application might have database records for:

  • Products and their prices.
  • Customer details.
  • Orders and payment information.
  • Available stock.

When a product is purchased, the application can update its stock information in the database. When someone searches for a product, the application retrieves the relevant record.

The takeaway: A database is an organised place to store and manage information, just like a supermarket needs organised records to manage its inventory.

☕ 6. Algorithm: Making the Perfect Masala Chai

Cooking is one of the best examples of an algorithm.

Imagine you want to make the perfect masala chai. You can't just randomly throw ingredients into a pan and hope for the best. You follow a sequence of steps.

  1. Pour water into a pan.
  2. Add tea leaves.
  3. Add crushed ginger and spices.
  4. Let the mixture boil for a certain amount of time.
  5. Add milk and sugar.
  6. Simmer, strain, and serve.

The order matters. The quantity of ingredients matters. Even the time spent boiling can change the final taste.

An algorithm works in a similar way. It is a clearly defined sequence of steps used to solve a problem or complete a task.

For example, a sorting algorithm follows a set of instructions to arrange numbers in ascending or descending order. A search algorithm follows a process to find a particular item in a collection.

Just as you can adjust your chai recipe to get the taste you want, algorithms can be designed and improved to solve problems more efficiently.

The takeaway: An algorithm is simply a step-by-step procedure for achieving a particular result.

🚌 7. Cloud Computing: Using a Public Bus

Imagine you need to travel from one place to another.

You could buy your own vehicle. But then you would have to pay for the vehicle, fuel, maintenance, insurance, and all the other expenses that come with owning it.

Or you could simply use a public bus. You pay for the journey you need, travel to your destination, and don't have to own or maintain the bus yourself.

Cloud computing follows a similar idea.

Instead of buying and maintaining all the servers and infrastructure yourself, you can use computing resources provided by a cloud service provider.

You can rent storage, processing power, databases, and other services according to your requirements. Depending on the service and pricing model, you may pay based on usage or a subscription.

For example, a business can host its website on a cloud platform instead of purchasing and maintaining its own physical servers.

The takeaway: Cloud computing lets you access computing resources over the internet without having to own and manage all the underlying infrastructure yourself.

🚶 8. Machine Learning: A Child Learning to Walk

Think about a toddler learning to walk.

The child tries to stand, takes a few steps, and falls. Then they get up and try again. They might fall several times, but gradually they learn how to balance, move their legs, and walk more confidently.

Machine learning has a similar idea behind it.

A model makes predictions using what it has learned from training data. During training, its predictions are evaluated, and its parameters are adjusted to improve its performance.

For example, imagine training a model to predict house prices. It makes predictions using information such as location, size, and number of rooms. The predictions are compared with actual prices, and the model is adjusted to reduce the error.

Just like the toddler gradually improves with practice, the model can improve through repeated training.

There is one important difference: machine learning models don't learn from experience in exactly the same way humans do. Their improvement comes from mathematical optimisation using data and feedback signals.

The takeaway: Machine learning involves improving a model's performance by learning patterns from examples and adjusting its parameters during training.

🧂 9. Cache: Ingredients Kept on the Kitchen Counter

Imagine you're cooking every day.

You frequently use salt, sugar, and cooking oil. Would you go to the storeroom every single time you needed a pinch of salt?

Probably not. You would keep frequently used ingredients somewhere easily accessible, like the kitchen counter.

That's how caching works.

A cache stores frequently accessed data in a location that can be accessed more quickly than the original source.

For example, when you visit a website, your browser may store certain resources, such as images, stylesheets, and scripts. When you visit the same website again, the browser may reuse those cached resources instead of downloading everything again.

This can make websites load faster and reduce unnecessary requests.

The takeaway: A cache keeps frequently needed information close at hand so it can be accessed faster.

📸 10. Data Backup: Keeping Duplicate Childhood Photos

Imagine you have a photograph from your childhood that means a lot to you.

You wouldn't want to lose it, right? After all, you can't go back in time and recreate that exact moment.

So you might keep a digital copy on your phone, another copy on your computer, and perhaps another in cloud storage.

Why? Because if one copy gets lost, damaged, or accidentally deleted, you still have another one.

That's the basic idea behind data backups.

A backup is an additional copy of important information that can be used to restore the original data if something goes wrong.

For example, a business might keep backups of its customer records, website files, and transaction history. If its main system fails, it can recover the information from a backup.

It's worth remembering that simply having duplicate files is not always enough. Good backup practices also involve storing copies separately, updating them regularly, and occasionally testing whether the data can actually be restored.

The takeaway: Backups protect important information by keeping additional copies that can be recovered when the original data is lost.

💡 Why Everyday Analogies Matter

These are just a few analogies I came up with, and honestly, thinking of them was a lot of fun!

Technology has so many concepts that can sound intimidating when explained using technical terminology. But when you connect them to something familiar, the same concepts become much easier to understand.

You don't need to explain a neural network using mathematical equations to someone who has never studied machine learning. You can start with a family preparing dinner. You don't need to explain cloud infrastructure through servers and data centres immediately. You can start with a public bus.

Of course, analogies are not exact technical definitions. Every analogy has its limitations, and the details become more nuanced when you explore the actual technology. But they are a wonderful starting point for building intuition.
