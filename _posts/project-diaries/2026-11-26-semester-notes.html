Semester Notes

November 26, 2026

Semester Notes is a personal project that is very close to my heart. It is a collection of my academic notes, carefully organized semester by semester and subject by subject. I created it primarily for myself, but eventually decided to make it accessible to others as well.

The idea was simple: bring all my study material together in one place, arrange everything properly, and make revision easier. What started as a simple collection of folders gradually turned into a much more organized project, complete with a website, a detailed GitHub README, and even a search feature.

"A place for everything, and everything in its place."

Inspiration Behind

"Some people collect things. I collect folders, notes, and beautifully organized directories."

Now, before getting into the technical details, let me tell you something about myself. I have always loved making notes. Not just writing them, but arranging them, organizing them, colour-coding them, and making them look presentable.

I genuinely enjoy the process of taking a bunch of information and turning it into something structured. Give me a notebook, some highlighters, and a few different coloured pens, and I can spend hours organizing things.

When I started studying Computer Science, I got introduced to something that made this habit even more interesting: computers, files, folders, and directory structures.

It was one of the first times I actually started using a laptop for my academic work. I learned how files were stored, how folders worked, and how everything could be arranged into different levels. And naturally, I took that idea a little too seriously.

My semester folder started looking something like this: 

Semester Notes/
│
├── 1 SEMESTER/
│   ├── Subject 1/
│   ├── Subject 2/
│   └── Subject 3/
│
├── 2 SEMESTER/
│   ├── Subject 1/
│   ├── Subject 2/
│   └── Subject 3/
│
├── 3 SEMESTER/
│   └── ...
│
└── 4 SEMESTER/
    └── ...

And that was just the beginning. Inside individual subject folders, I had even more folders. There were separate folders for laboratory files, experiments, code, projects, documents, and other study materials.

I remember submitting a lab file once, and the teacher happened to look at the file path. The path was so long that it became a little funny. It was basically:

Semester Notes / 1 SEMESTER / Subject Name / Lab / Experiments / Experiment Folder / Code...

And honestly, what was the problem? I was happy with it! Everything had its own place, and I knew exactly where to find it.

Looking back, I think this project was a natural extension of that habit. I already loved making notes and organizing them, so why not turn the whole thing into a proper project?

The Art of Making Notes

"The notes you make today might save you hours of confusion tomorrow."

One thing I learned while studying is that you cannot always predict what you will remember later. Something that seems extremely obvious when you are learning a topic for the first time might not be obvious at all when you revisit it months later.

That is why I try to include even the seemingly obvious details in my notes. Sometimes, you need to write down the little things too, because those are often the things you forget.

This is especially important when studying a subject for the first time. You are building your understanding from scratch, and the explanations that make sense to you at that moment might become surprisingly difficult to recall later.

Obvious Information (Source)

I also have a very particular relationship with the appearance of my notes. At the beginning of a semester, everything looks beautiful. The headings are neat, the bullet points are properly arranged, and the colours are perfectly coordinated.

But as the semester progresses, something interesting happens. The notes slowly become less organized, the handwriting gets more questionable, and the pages start collecting random doodles.

The beginning of the semester and the end of the semester look like they belong to two completely different people.

The Beginning vs. The End of a Semester (Source)

I even had a rough copy and a main copy. The main copy was where I tried to keep everything neat, organized, and presentable. The rough copy, on the other hand, was a completely different story.

It was the place where I could write whatever I wanted, make mistakes, scribble things down, and stop worrying about how the page looked. Basically, it was where all the remaining motivation to write notes went when the semester got too overwhelming.

So, yes, this project is not just about storing academic material. It is also a reflection of how I study, how I make notes, and how much I enjoy keeping things organized.

Building the Project

"What starts as a folder on your laptop can eventually become something much bigger."

Initially, Semester Notes was just a collection of folders on my computer. I kept adding notes as I studied, arranging them according to their respective semesters and subjects.

Over time, the collection grew. There were more subjects, more documents, more laboratory files, and more resources to keep track of. Eventually, I wanted a better way to access everything.

That is when I decided to turn the collection into a website.

The idea was to create a simple interface where I could browse through semesters, find individual subjects, and access the corresponding study material without having to manually search through a large number of folders.

I also added a search feature so that finding something would become even easier. Instead of navigating through every directory, I could simply search for what I needed.

Another part of the project that I particularly enjoyed was creating the GitHub README. I wanted it to be more than just a short introduction. I tried to make it informative, detailed, and useful enough that someone visiting the repository could understand the entire structure without having to explore every folder manually.

The README: A Project Within a Project

"If you have organized everything beautifully, why not make the instructions beautiful too?"

One of my favourite parts of this project was working on the GitHub README. I did not want it to be just a few lines explaining what the repository contained. I wanted it to act as a proper guide.

So I created a detailed table of contents with separate sections for each semester. Every subject has a direct link to its respective folder, and where applicable, I also included links to the corresponding laboratory projects.

I tried to make the README informative enough that anyone visiting the repository could understand its structure and navigate through the available material without getting lost.

It was also a learning experience in itself. I explored how to structure a repository, organize directories, compress files, write documentation, and use links to connect related resources.

I especially enjoyed making the README more detailed over time. It felt satisfying to have not only the notes organized but also a clear explanation of where everything was located.

Basically, I wanted the organization to make sense both inside the folders and outside them.

Website and Features

"The best way to organize information is to make it easy to access."

Once the notes and repository structure were in place, I wanted to make the collection accessible through a website.

The website provides another way to explore the material. Instead of opening GitHub and navigating through several folders, visitors can browse the semester-wise collection through a more accessible interface.

I also added a search feature to make finding specific notes and subjects more convenient. It was a useful addition because the collection continued to grow, and manually searching through everything would eventually become inconvenient.

The website is hosted using GitHub Pages, which makes the project accessible online.

What I Learned

"Sometimes, a personal project teaches you more than you expected."

This project began as a way to organize my study material, but along the way, I learned quite a few things about managing and presenting information.

I became more comfortable with directory structures, organizing files, compressing folders, and maintaining a structured repository. I also explored website development, JavaScript, and the process of making information accessible through a search feature.

Working on the README helped me understand the importance of documentation. A project is not only about the files and code inside it; it is also about making everything understandable for someone who visits it.

Most importantly, I learned how a small personal habit can turn into something useful when you decide to build upon it.

Conclusion

Semester Notes is one of those projects that grew naturally out of something I already loved doing. I have always enjoyed making notes, arranging information, and keeping things organized. This project simply gave that habit a more structured form.

What started as folders on my laptop gradually became a complete collection of semester-wise notes, a detailed GitHub repository, and a website that makes everything easier to access.

It is also a little personal archive of my academic journey. Every semester adds something new, and every subject brings another collection of notes, files, and memories.

If you are a student looking for organized study material, or someone interested in exploring how I structured my academic notes, feel free to visit the project.

You can find the complete repository on GitHub and browse the notes through the Semester Notes Website.

And if there is one thing this project has taught me, it is that being a little too fond of organizing things is not necessarily a bad thing. Sometimes, it leads to something genuinely useful!
